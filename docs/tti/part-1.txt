Introduction: The Shape of Innity

            To be read before all the others.

    Every cosmology begins somewhere improbable. This one began in sleep  with a wheel
of coloured segments, red and white and black and grey, turning and collapsing toward a single
point at the corner of vision, each segment shrinking and crowding closer as it spiralled inward
until, at the centre, the edges dissolved entirely and there was only the point. On waking, the
natural thing was to want to see it again, and so it was rebuilt as an animation. Then the less
natural thing happened. Watching the wheel turn, a question surfaced that had nothing to do
with the image and everything to do with what the image was secretly a diagram of  the
oldest question there is, and the one we have learned to stop asking because it seems to have no
foothold: why is there anything here at all?

    Physics, for all its staggering precision, does not touch this question. It tells us with breath-
taking accuracy how the universe behaves  but every physical theory, without exception, opens
its account with the curtain already raised: a stage already standing, laws already in force, elds
already humming, something already present for the equations to be about. Why there is a stage,
why there are laws, why there is anything rather than the seamless nothing that would have
been so much simpler  this is left entirely untouched, set aside as metaphysics, or as a ques-
tion malformed from the start. The framework set out in this volume refuses that surrender.
It attempts to answer the prior question  not with new physics, not with a deeper particle
or an earlier instant, but with geometry: with nothing more than the relationship between two
numbers.

    Take zero and one. Nothing in mathematics looks more ordinary, and nothing hides more.
Zero is not merely nothing; it is the oor no shrinking quantity ever reaches, approached forever
and never arrived at  an innity opening downward, into the small. One is the edge of the
measurable, the boundary of the unit, the threshold of this rather than that. And between these
two unremarkable markers lies something stranger than either of them alone: the interval from
zero to one contains more numbers than the entire unending march of the counting numbers
1, 2, 3, 4, . . . put together.   Cantor proved it, rigorously and unarguably.   The small innity
nested between zero and one is, in a precise mathematical sense, vastly larger and denser than
the large innity that runs to the stars. The framework takes this asymmetry not as a curiosity
but as a structural fact about reality  a tilt built into the foundations, whose consequences
return, much later, as the direction of time itself.

    Now ask the decisive question: how are zero and one joined?          Draw a straight line from
the one toward the innitely small, and it never closes  it runs on forever, resolving nothing,
producing nothing nite, nothing that could stand and stay. For the relationship between the
two to close into something real, the path between them cannot run straight.          It must curve.
And the constant that governs all curving, the lone ratio that bends a line into a circle and lets
a path return to itself, is π . Without π there are no circles; without closure, the path between
the two innities never comes back, and nothing whatsoever can exist. In this reading π is not

Now take only half that journey. Halve the π . What falls out is not a smaller, tamer quantity but

It opens the seed of a wholly new dimension  i, the operator that lifts the real number line

before. Fold that new perpendicular onto the old familiar line, and the complex plane springs
into being.
   And this is the hinge on which everything turns, because the complex plane is not a conve-
nient abstraction with no address in the world. It is the native country of quantum mechanics.
Every wavefunction  the mathematical object that, in the standard account, does not merely
describe a particle but in a real sense is the particle  is complex-valued. It cannot be written
down without i. This is no mere notational habit: experiments in quantum information have
now demonstrated that a quantum theory stripped of its imaginary axis makes dierent and
worse predictions than our universe actually obeys. The imaginary dimension is not decoration;
it is load-bearing. And so the chain closes, link by link, with a kind of vertigo: zero and one
demand π ; π halved gives i; i unfolds the complex plane; the complex plane houses the wave-
function; the wavefunction is matter. Existence, the framework proposes, is not a property the
universe happened to be granted. It is the shape zero and one are forced to make when they
close the distance between them through the only constant capable of closing it at all.
   But a universe of awlessly described matter could still be utterly dark within  entirely
real, and aware of nothing. So the framework turns, in its second movement, to the question
physics most conspicuously cannot reach: where does experience enter? Its answer is again a
single symbol, this time drawn from calculus  dx, the innitesimal step.        A single moment
