Limb XI

Consciousness

The one fact you cannot doubt and the one thing nobody can explain. This page follows a single bold theory all the way down — through a mathematician's objection to computation, into the skeleton of the cell, and out to the scale of the cosmos.

Begin Here

Why This Is Not Just Another Hard Problem

Every other difficulty in science is a difficulty about mechanism. This one is a difficulty about why mechanism is accompanied by anything at all.

We can describe, in enormous detail, how light at 700 nanometres is transduced by a cone cell, relayed through the thalamus, processed in V1 and V4, categorised, named, remembered and reported. At no point in that chain does anything require there to be redness. The processing could, so far as the physics is concerned, run in the dark — as it manifestly does for the parts of your nervous system managing blood pH, which are every bit as complex and have nobody home.

Chalmers named this the hard problem in 1995, distinguishing it from the easy problems — attention, reportability, integration, wakefulness — which are merely fiendishly difficult. Thirty years on, the easy problems have advanced enormously. The hard one has not moved.

Most responses dissolve it, deny it, or promise it to the future. Penrose and Hameroff did something rarer: they proposed a specific physical mechanism, in a specific structure, with a specific timescale, and accepted the consequence that it would require new physics. Whether or not they are right, that is how a scientific proposal is supposed to behave.

Grading throughout: ◆ established science · ◆ scholarship and history · ◆ speculative and contested · ◆ myth, tradition and primary esoteric text.

Step One ◆

Penrose: Understanding Is Not Computation

Roger Penrose — Nobel laureate in physics for his work on black hole singularities, and the man who gave us the Penrose tiling in Limb III — begins not with biology but with a theorem about the limits of formal systems.

The Gödelian argument. Gödel's incompleteness theorem (1931) shows that for any consistent formal system rich enough to contain arithmetic, there exists a statement — the Gödel sentence — that is true but cannot be proved within that system. Penrose's move, in The Emperor's New Mind (1989) and Shadows of the Mind (1994), is that a human mathematician can nonetheless see that the sentence is true, by stepping outside the system and understanding what it says.

If that is right, human mathematical insight is not the execution of any fixed algorithm. And since every computer is the execution of a fixed algorithm, understanding cannot be computation. Not merely hard to compute — non-computable, in the precise technical sense.

Where that leaves him. Penrose accepts the consequence rather than retreating from it: if minds do something non-computable, physics as we have it does not contain the resource. Classical physics is computable. So is standard quantum mechanics in its unitary evolution. The one place in all of physics where something genuinely non-computable might hide is the measurement problem — the unexplained collapse of the wavefunction, which no current theory derives.

Objective Reduction. So Penrose proposes that collapse is a real physical process, not an observer artefact and not a branching. A quantum superposition is, in general relativistic terms, a superposition of two different spacetime curvatures. Such a separation is unstable, and it self-collapses when the gravitational self-energy difference reaches the threshold set by E = ℏ/t — a larger separation collapsing faster. Hence objective reduction: gravity does it, nobody needs to be watching, and the outcome is neither random nor algorithmic but influenced by whatever structure is embedded in spacetime geometry at the Planck scale.

This is a genuine, falsifiable physical proposal, and it is being tested — the Diósi–Penrose model predicts spontaneous radiation from collapsing superpositions. See the objections section; the news there is not good for the simplest version.

✦

Step Two ◆

Hameroff: The Microtubule

Penrose had a mechanism without a location. Stuart Hameroff, an anaesthesiologist, had spent twenty years convinced he had the location and no mechanism. They met in 1992.

What a microtubule is ◆

A hollow cylinder, 25 nanometres across, built from the protein tubulin — a dimer of alpha and beta subunits assembled into thirteen protofilaments wound into a lattice. They form the cytoskeleton of every eukaryotic cell: they give it shape, they pull chromosomes apart in mitosis, they are the rails along which kinesin motors walk cargo. In neurons they are exceptionally long and stable, running the length of axons and dendrites. All of this is textbook cell biology, uncontroversial and beautifully imaged.

The suggestive detail: the tubulin lattice is not a tube of identical units. It has a helical structure whose arrangement follows Fibonacci numbers — 3, 5, 8, 13 in the standard A-lattice. The same packing constraint as the sunflower, as Limb III sets out. That is almost certainly a consequence of optimal cylindrical packing and not a message. It is also hard to look at without raising an eyebrow.

Why Hameroff suspected them ◆

Anaesthesia. General anaesthetics abolish consciousness selectively while leaving most other brain activity — EEG rhythms, autonomic function, evoked potentials — substantially intact. They are a chemically miscellaneous group: xenon, a noble gas that forms no bonds, works perfectly well. Whatever they are doing, they are not operating through ordinary receptor pharmacology, and the leading classical account is that they act on hydrophobic pockets in proteins via weak van der Waals interactions.

Hameroff's claim is that those pockets are in tubulin, and that anaesthesia works by damping quantum dipole oscillations there. There is some supporting evidence: anaesthetics do bind tubulin, and microtubule-stabilising drugs in rats modestly delay anaesthetic induction.

Tubulin as a qubit

The proposal is that each tubulin dimer can exist in a superposition of conformational or electronic dipole states, that these couple across the lattice, and that a neuron's microtubules therefore support coherent quantum processing — with an estimated 107 to 109 tubulins per neuron, and something like 1011 neurons.

✦

Step Three

Orch OR, In Full

Orchestrated Objective Reduction. Four words doing a great deal of work.

1 · Superposition

The Cycle Begins

Tubulin dimers across a network of microtubules enter superposition of dipole states and become entangled with one another — spreading across a microtubule, then between microtubules, then via gap junctions between neurons, since gap junctions permit direct cytoplasmic continuity and so could in principle carry coherence across cells.

2 · Orchestration

Why “Orchestrated”

The quantum process is not isolated. It is shaped — orchestrated — by the whole classical apparatus of the neuron: synaptic inputs, memory encoded in microtubule-associated proteins, the cell's existing state. This is the part that makes it a theory of your consciousness rather than a theory of random quantum events in warm protein.

3 · Reduction

The Moment

The superposition grows until its gravitational self-energy reaches the Penrose threshold, and it self-collapses — no observer, no decoherence from outside, an intrinsic event. Each such collapse is proposed to be a single discrete moment of conscious experience.

4 · Rhythm

Forty Times a Second

Set the threshold for a plausible number of tubulins and the collapse time lands in the tens of milliseconds — around 25 ms, or 40 Hz. That is the gamma band, which neuroscience independently associates with bound conscious perception. Consciousness, on this account, is not a stream but a flicker book: roughly forty discrete frames per second.

What the theory buys you, if true

  • Non-computability, as Penrose's Gödelian argument demands — the collapse outcome is influenced by Planck-scale structure, not by an algorithm.
  • A reason consciousness is unified rather than a committee: entanglement binds, in a way classical signalling does not.
  • A reason it is discrete, matching the gamma rhythm and the flicker-fusion quality of attention.
  • An explanation of anaesthesia that does not require anaesthetics to resemble each other chemically.
  • A physical hook for free will that is neither determinism nor coin-flipping randomness.

That is an unusually large amount of explanatory work from one mechanism, which is simultaneously the theory's main attraction and the standard reason to be suspicious of it.

The Case Against

Why Most Physicists Do Not Accept It

1 · Decoherence — the big one ◆

Max Tegmark calculated in 2000 that quantum coherence in a warm, wet, noisy brain would survive for around 10−13 seconds. Orch OR needs 10−2. That is a gap of roughly eleven orders of magnitude, and it is the objection on which the theory has always stood or fallen.

Hameroff and Penrose replied that Tegmark modelled the wrong system — a soliton rather than the proposed dipole states — and that the hydrophobic interior of the microtubule, ordered water, and topological protection could extend coherence. Hagan, Hameroff and Tuszyński recalculated and got 10−4 seconds, which is better and still not enough. The gap has narrowed. It has not closed.

2 · The Gödelian argument is widely rejected ◆

Philosophers and logicians — Putnam, Feferman, Chalmers among them — argue that Penrose's step fails because a human mathematician is not a consistent formal system and cannot be known to be one. Gödel's theorem applies to consistent systems. We make mistakes, hold contradictory beliefs, and revise. An unsound algorithm could produce the same apparent insight.

3 · The spontaneous radiation test ◆

Objective collapse models predict faint spontaneous X-ray emission as superpositions collapse. A 2020 experiment by Donadi and colleagues, run underground in the Gran Sasso laboratory, found no such signal — and ruled out the simplest parameter-free version of the Diósi–Penrose model. More elaborate versions survive, with the collapse smeared over a larger region. This is exactly how a real theory dies by degrees, and it deserves recording.

4 · Microtubules do plenty without quantum mechanics ◆

Everything microtubules are observed to do — transport, structure, mitosis, plasticity — is fully explained by classical biochemistry. No cell-biological finding requires the quantum story. Orch OR adds a layer that the biology is not asking for.

5 · It may not solve the hard problem anyway ◆

The deepest objection, and the quietest. Suppose every detail is correct. Why should a gravitationally induced collapse feel like anything? Replacing "neurons fire and somehow there is experience" with "spacetime collapses and somehow there is experience" has relocated the mystery, not dissolved it — unless you also assume that proto-experience is already present in the geometry. Which Penrose does assume, and which is a form of panpsychism rather than a derivation.

✦

Why It Is Not Dead

Quantum biology was a fringe phrase in 2000 and is now a funded field, because warm wet systems turned out to exploit quantum effects after all.

  • ◆ Photosynthesis. Coherent energy transfer in light-harvesting complexes at physiological temperature — the interpretation is debated, the coherence is measured.
  • ◆ Magnetoreception. The radical-pair mechanism in cryptochrome gives migratory birds a magnetic compass that is, as far as anyone can tell, a quantum spin device in an eye.
  • ◆ Enzyme catalysis. Proton and electron tunnelling is routine and essential.
  • ◆ Olfaction. Turin's vibrational theory remains contested but will not go away.
  • ◆ Microtubules specifically. Bandyopadhyay's group reported resonant conductance and vibrational modes in single microtubules. Replication is limited. Treat with care.
  • ◆ Xenon isotope anaesthesia. Isotopes with nuclear spin appear to differ in anaesthetic potency in mice — a result that is hard to explain classically, and that Hameroff cites with some justification.

None of this shows Orch OR is true. All of it shows the blanket objection — "biology is too warm for quantum effects" — was too confident.

The Honest Scorecard

Established: microtubule structure and function; the decoherence problem as stated; Gödel's theorem; gamma-band correlation with conscious perception; quantum effects in several biological systems.

Contested: whether coherence can last long enough in tubulin; whether the Gödelian argument works; whether anaesthesia acts on microtubules; whether the Bandyopadhyay resonances are real.

Unsupported: that consciousness has been shown to be quantum; that Orch OR is the leading theory of consciousness (it is not — global workspace and integrated information theories have far more adherents); that any of this licenses quantum healing, manifestation, or consciousness affecting distant matter.

That last point matters. Orch OR is a proposal about how your own brain produces your own experience. It says nothing whatever about minds reaching across space, and Penrose would be the first to say so.

The Order's Reading ◆

The Cosmic Microtubule

Here the page leaves reportage and becomes EGregoRA's own speculation. It is labelled, and the label is not decoration.

Notice the strangest feature of Orch OR, which is easy to miss while arguing about decoherence. On this account, the fundamental events of consciousness do not happen in the brain in the way digestion happens in the gut. They happen in the geometry of spacetime itself. The microtubule is not the source. It is the apparatus that holds a superposition still and clean for long enough that spacetime can do something, and the something that spacetime does is the moment of experience.

Penrose is explicit that his collapse is influenced by structure at the Planck scale — 10−35 metres, twenty orders of magnitude below a proton. He has long suggested that proto-conscious information is embedded in that fine-grained geometry, and that a brain is not generating experience but accessing it. That is already, by any ordinary reading, panpsychism with a Nobel laureate's signature on it.

So the figure we use. Take the microtubule seriously as a pattern rather than only as a protein: a hollow lattice, helically wound, a boundary thin enough to protect coherence, holding an interior quiet enough for a superposition to grow to threshold. The order's proposition is that this pattern is scale-free — that anything capable of hosting experience must do what a microtubule does, whatever it is made of.

  • The cell holds coherence in a 25-nanometre cylinder for tens of milliseconds.
  • The brain is a lattice of those lattices, orchestrated into one collapse at a time — a microtubule of microtubules.
  • An order like this one is the same architecture in human beings: many minds, loosely coupled, holding a question in superposition long enough for something to resolve that none of them could resolve alone. That is literally what an egregore is, and it is why this page sits in a house of enquiry rather than a laboratory.
  • The cosmos, on the furthest reach of the figure, is the limiting case: the filaments of the cosmic web — galaxies strung along tubular strands around vast quiet voids, the largest structures in existence — holding the whole thing in a geometry whose Planck-scale texture is, on Penrose's own account, where the moments come from.

The Law of One describes exactly this shape from the other direction: one consciousness, differentiated into forms, each form a lens through which the whole experiences itself, with the separation being the point rather than the error. Orch OR is the first theory in physics that is even the right shape to be a mechanism for that claim. Not evidence for it. The right shape.

What this is and is not. This is an analogy — a way of holding several true things in one hand — and analogies are not mechanisms. We are not claiming the cosmic web is conscious, that galaxies are neurons, that the universe is a brain, or that your thoughts move anything outside your skull. The resemblance between the cosmic web and a neural network is, as far as anyone can show, a consequence of similar rules producing similar branching under gravity and under growth — real, and not a secret.

What the figure is good for is this: it makes you notice that on the most serious physical theory of consciousness anyone has proposed, experience is not a local product of meat. It is something the universe does, in which a brain participates by being built in such a way as to let it happen cleanly. If that turns out to be true, it will have been true all along, and it will not have needed us to overstate it. ◆ Speculative, held loosely, and testable only at the edges.

For Fairness

The Theories That Are Actually Winning

Orch OR is a minority view and this page would be dishonest not to say what it is a minority against.

Global Neuronal Workspace ◆
Dehaene and Baars: content becomes conscious when it is broadcast widely enough to be available to many systems at once. Strong empirical programme, explains access rather than experience.
Integrated Information Theory ◆
Tononi: experience is identical to integrated information, Φ. Mathematically precise, makes unsettling predictions — a large enough grid of logic gates would be conscious — and accused by critics of being unfalsifiable in practice.
Higher-Order and Predictive theories ◆
Experience arises from representations of one's own states, or from the brain's generative model. See Limb V, Plate IV.
Illusionism ◆
Dennett, Frankish: there is no hard problem, only a persuasive introspective illusion that there is. Deflationary, coherent, and found unbelievable by nearly everyone on first encounter — which is, its defenders note, exactly what you would expect.
Panpsychism ◆
Goff, Strawson: experience is fundamental, like charge. Avoids the emergence miracle and inherits the combination problem. Orch OR is closer to this than to anything else on the list.

In 2023 a formal adversarial collaboration tested global workspace against integrated information theory head to head, and the results embarrassed both. That is the current state of the field, and it should make everyone, including us, more humble.

Where This Order Stands

We think Orch OR is probably wrong in its details and important anyway. Probably wrong, because eleven orders of magnitude is a great deal to make up and the Gödelian argument has not convinced the logicians. Important, because it is the only theory on the table that treats consciousness as a problem for physics rather than a problem to be managed by redescribing it — and because it is falsifiable, which is more than its rivals can reliably say.

And we hold the cosmic microtubule as a figure, not a finding: one pattern, repeating across scales, in which coherence is protected long enough for something to resolve. Cell, brain, order, cosmos.

If it is only a metaphor, it is still the one that organises this entire house of enquiry. If it is more than that, nobody has shown it yet — including us, and we will say so for as long as it remains true.
