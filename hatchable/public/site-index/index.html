Plates: The Vesica. Two circles, each through the other's centre. The almond between has a height-to-width ratio of exactly √3, and from it the equilateral triangle, hexagon and square follow with compass and straightedge alone. Everything else on this page begins here. ◆ Provable. · Seed and Flower. Six circles around a seventh — the densest packing of equal circles in a plane, proved by Thue in 1890. ◆ The packing is physics. ◆ The Flower of Life as a blueprint of creation is myth — a magnificent one, scorched into granite at the Osireion at Abydos and copied for three millennia since. · The Five. Euclid closes the Elements with this proof, and it remains one of the most satisfying objects in thought: not five we have found — five that can be. ◆ Proven. Plato's elemental assignment is ◆ myth, and the dodecahedron-as-cosmos is the tell: it was the one left over. · 137.5°. Rational divergence angles close the loop and make rays; irrational ones never repeat. Douady and Couder reproduced sunflower spirals using repelling ferrofluid droplets — no genetics in the system at all. ◆ Science. φ in the Parthenon, in credit cards, in faces — ◆ mostly myth, built by choosing where to lay the ruler. · The Great Failure. Twenty-four centuries of attempts, ended by Lindemann's proof that π is transcendental. ◆ Settled. It belongs on this page because the tradition's most famous quest was answered with a flat no — and because every modern claim that some monument squares the circle is describing an approximation, which is a different and much smaller marvel. · Penrose & the Quasicrystal. Crystallography proved five-fold symmetry impossible in a repeating lattice — then Penrose tiled the plane with it anyway, non-repeating, using two rhombs whose ratio is φ. In 1982 Shechtman found the same arrangement in an alloy, was told he was wrong for a decade, and collected the Nobel Prize in 2011. ◆ Science, and a parable: the forbidden pattern was real, and the way to establish it was measurement, not insistence. · The Wallpaper Groups. There are seventeen ways to repeat a pattern across a plane — no more, and the proof is nineteenth-century. The craftsmen of the Alhambra found almost all of them by eye, centuries early. ◆ Proven, and independently discovered by hand — which is the strongest evidence that the tradition was tracking something real rather than imagining it. · The Recursive Tree. A single substitution rule, applied five times, with no plan of the whole. This is how Lindenmayer modelled plant growth in 1968, and it is close to how plants actually do it: local rules, no blueprint. ◆ Science. The lesson generalises — complexity here is evidence of a simple rule iterated, not of a designer's drawing. · Why Exactly Five. At any vertex of a convex solid the faces must sum to less than 360°, or the corner lies flat and will not close. Fan regular polygons around a point and count: triangles work at three, four and five and fail at six; squares work at three and fail at four; pentagons work at three and fail at four; hexagons fail at three. Eight arrangements are even conceivable and five survive. ◆ This is Euclid, Elements XIII.18, and it is a closed question — not five because five is beautiful, five because space in three dimensions permits no sixth. · The Most Irrational Number. Both heads use one rule: place each seed at a fixed angle from the last. The left uses 137.507°, the right 137.300°. A fifth of a degree opens visible rifts, because φ's continued fraction is [1;1,1,1,…] — all ones, the slowest-converging expansion there is, so its angle is the hardest of all to approximate by a rational and therefore the last to fall into repeating rays. ◆ Hurwitz's theorem, 1891, makes that precise. The plant is not counting. It is simply the only angle that never lines up. · Five Will Not Tile. Only 2-, 3-, 4- and 6-fold rotations are compatible with a lattice — the crystallographic restriction theorem, provable in a paragraph. Five pentagons around a point leave 36° over. Penrose's two rhombs (1974) fill the plane with five-fold order and no period at all, and in April 1982 Dan Shechtman saw exactly that diffraction pattern in a rapidly cooled aluminium–manganese alloy. ◆ He was told to reread his textbook, left his research group, and took the Nobel Prize in 2011. · Plateau's Laws. Soap films always meet three to an edge at exactly 120°, and those edges meet four to a point at 109.47°. Joseph Plateau wrote the rules down in 1873 from observation; Jean Taylor proved them in 1976. For equal areas in a plane the least-perimeter partition is the regular hexagon — conjectured by Pappus around 300 CE and ◆ finally proved by Thomas Hales in 1999. The bee is not a geometer. The surface tension is.

Plates: The Tree of Stars. The oak and the constellation drawn as one figure — the order's founding intuition in a single image: that the thing overhead and the thing in the field are continuous, and that only our departments say otherwise. ◆ Emblem. No claim is made that branching in trees and filamentary structure in the cosmos share a cause; they arise from quite different physics, and the resemblance is the point of the picture rather than evidence for anything. · The Inner Cosmos. For Limb V. The observer as part of the observed — a nervous system modelling a universe that produced it, with the geometry of the model and the geometry of the thing drawn in the same gold. ◆ Emblem, and a deliberately double-edged one: the visionary state does not grant access to the cosmos. It alters the instrument. Both halves of that sentence are in the picture. · Solve et Coagula. For Limb X, in the manner of the emblem books — Maier's Atalanta Fugiens and the Mutus Liber, which taught by picture because the pictures were the instruction. ◆ Scholarship in form, ◆ myth in content: the four elements as states and tendencies, the four stages of the opus as the arc of coming apart and reassembling. Nothing here is a recipe. · Solstice Alignment. For Limb II. The one picture here depicting something literally true: megalithic monuments really are aligned to solar events, and at Newgrange and Maeshowe the light really does enter the chamber on the solstice. ◆ Archaeoastronomy. What those builders believed is unrecoverable; that they measured the sky with extraordinary care is not in doubt.
