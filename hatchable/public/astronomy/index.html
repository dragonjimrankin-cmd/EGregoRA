Limb I · i — a sub-limb of Cosmic Physics

Astronomy: The Survey of the Sky

The oldest science and the only one whose laboratory cannot be entered. Everything on this page was worked out by sitting still, looking up, and being extremely careful about light.

Begin Here

The Discipline of Looking Up

Astronomy is the order's oldest limb and its strictest. It is where the mystical and the measurable have the longest shared border, and where the border is best policed.

No astronomer has ever touched a star. Everything known about the composition, the mass, the age, the motion and the fate of every object beyond the solar system was extracted from light that arrived here on its own, was split into a spectrum, and was counted. That this works at all is one of the genuinely astonishing facts about the universe: matter everywhere emits and absorbs at the same frequencies as matter here, so a dark line in a star's spectrum names an element across ten billion years and ten billion light years. ◆ established science.

The order keeps astronomy and astrology in separate rooms on purpose. Astrology is studied here as a symbolic technology with a real history and no predictive power over the physical; astronomy is the measurement. Both are honest work; confusing them is not.

Grading throughout: ◆ established science · ◆ scholarship and history · ◆ speculative and contested · ◆ myth, tradition and primary esoteric text.

The Instrument

The Ladder of Scale

One map, nineteen rungs, twenty-six powers of ten: from the face of the Sun out to the edge of the observable universe — and then one turn further, past where measurement can go, to the order's own figure for the shape of the whole. Drag it, wheel it, or use the slider; the arrow keys work once the map has focus.

Across the view

How it cheats, and why. Screen radius is the logarithm of real radius: one ring out is ten times further, not ten pixels further. Drawn truthfully, with the Sun one pixel across, the observable universe would be some ten thousand billion pixels wide. Every distance quoted in the readout and in the ladder below is real; only the spacing on the glass is compressed. ◆ established science, logarithmically drawn — out to the horizon. The last two rungs are not measurements, and the map changes what it draws when it reaches them.

The Ladder, Written Out

The same seventeen rungs in plain numbers — and the list is live: click a rung and the map goes there.

The Last Two Rungs

Nineteen of the twenty-one rungs are measurements. The last two are not, and the map says so by changing what it draws: the rings and the star field fade, and a torus with a single half-twisted band running round it fades in, with the whole observable universe marked on the band as one small bright disc.

Beyond the horizon is an inference. Inflation implies the whole is very much larger than the part inside our light cone — conservatively by a factor of a thousand, possibly without limit — and no observation made from in here can ever settle it. ◆ speculative and contested.

The Moebius Torus is the order's own emblem, the same seam the atlas strings its limbs on: a torus seamed like a Möbius band, so that what leaves by one face returns by the other and there is no outside to stand in. Its one-way flow is causality; what comes round is the cosmic ledger. What cosmology actually measures is the curvature of space, which is flat to within a few tenths of a per cent — a result that permits several topologies, including a finite one that wraps, and proves none of them. The drawing is an emblem and is filed as one — and it is set out in full, with its own refutations, at Limb I·iii. ◆ myth, tradition and primary esoteric text.

Limb I · i — First Subject

What a Star Is, and Which Kinds There Are

A star is a ball of plasma in which gravity pulling in and radiation pushing out have agreed to a truce. The whole of stellar astronomy is the study of how that truce is kept, and how it ends.

Spectral Classes ◆

O, B, A, F, G, K, M

The sequence is by surface temperature, from roughly 40,000 K down to 2,400 K, and it is an accident of history that the letters are not alphabetical: Annie Jump Cannon reordered Pickering's hydrogen-line classes at Harvard around 1901 when it became clear that temperature, not hydrogen strength, was the real variable.

  • O — blue, 30,000 K and up, a hundred thousand times the Sun's output, dead within a few million years. Perhaps one star in three million.
  • B — blue-white; Rigel, Spica.
  • A — white; Sirius, Vega, Deneb.
  • F — yellow-white; Procyon, Polaris.
  • G — yellow; the Sun, 5,772 K, Alpha Centauri A. About 7% of stars.
  • K — orange; Arcturus, Epsilon Eridani. Long-lived and increasingly the favourite place to look for life.
  • M — red dwarfs, under 3,700 K; three quarters of every star in the galaxy, and not one of them visible to the naked eye.

Below M run the brown dwarfs — classes L, T and Y — which never reached the 0.08 solar masses needed to fuse hydrogen. A Y dwarf can be cooler than a cup of tea. ◆

Lives ◆

How Long a Star Gets

Lifetime goes roughly as mass divided by luminosity, and luminosity goes as something near the 3.5th power of mass. The arithmetic is merciless: doubling the mass of a star cuts its life by a factor of about ten.

  • A 60-solar-mass O star: three million years.
  • The Sun, one solar mass: ten billion years, of which 4.6 are gone.
  • A 0.1-solar-mass red dwarf: trillions of years — longer than the present age of the universe by two orders of magnitude, so not one has ever died.

That last fact has a consequence worth sitting with. The universe is 13.8 billion years old and the most common kind of star in it is still in its infancy. If life is a thing that takes time, the universe has barely started. ◆ speculative and contested — a reasonable inference, not a result.

Ends ◆

Three Doors Out

What a star leaves behind is set almost entirely by the mass of its core when fusion stops.

  • Under ~8 solar masses — it swells to a red giant, sheds its outer layers as a planetary nebula (named by Herschel, nothing to do with planets) and leaves a white dwarf: a carbon-oxygen cinder the size of the Earth, held up by electron degeneracy pressure, cooling for the rest of time. Limit 1.4 solar masses, Chandrasekhar, 1930 — worked out at nineteen, on the boat to England.
  • 8 to ~25 solar masses — core collapse, a type II supernova, and a neutron star: 1.4 to 2.2 solar masses inside a ball 20 km across, spinning up to 716 times a second. A sugar cube of it weighs as much as a mountain.
  • Above that — nothing stops the collapse. A black hole, treated in its own section below.

Every atom in you heavier than helium was made inside one of these objects or in the collision of two of them. That is not a metaphor; it is nucleosynthesis. ◆

Variables and Pairs ◆

Stars That Change, and Stars in Company

Most stars are not alone. Something like half of Sun-like stars are in binaries or higher multiples, and the figure rises with mass; among O stars, nearly all.

  • Cepheid variables pulse with a period set by their luminosity — Henrietta Swan Leavitt, 1908, from 1,777 variables in the Magellanic Clouds. This is the rung of the distance ladder that let Hubble measure Andromeda and end the Great Debate.
  • RR Lyrae stars do the same job for older populations.
  • Eclipsing binaries give masses and radii directly, which is where the whole mass scale is anchored.
  • Novae are a white dwarf stealing hydrogen from a companion until the stolen layer detonates. The dwarf survives and does it again.
  • Type Ia supernovae are the same theft taken to the Chandrasekhar limit. They go off at nearly the same brightness every time, which makes them standard candles — and which is how the acceleration of the expansion was found in 1998.

Second Subject

Solar Systems, Ours and the Others

For the whole of human history we had one example. Since 1995 we have had nearly six thousand, and almost nothing about our own arrangement turns out to be typical.

Our Own ◆

One Star, Eight Planets, a Great Deal of Rubble

Four small rocky worlds inside the frost line; four giants outside it — two gas (Jupiter, Saturn) and two ice (Uranus, Neptune, which are mostly water, ammonia and methane under crushing pressure, and should never have been called gas giants). Between and beyond: the asteroid belt, the Kuiper belt, the scattered disc, the Oort cloud.

The Sun is 99.86% of the mass. Jupiter is most of the rest, and its position has shaped everything — probably including the delivery of water to the Earth and the architecture of the inner system. Pluto was reclassified in 2006 not out of spite but because Eris was found and the choice was to have eight planets or an indefinite number.

The Census ◆

Nearly Six Thousand Others

51 Pegasi b, 1995: a Jupiter-mass planet four days from its star. Nobody had a theory that allowed it, and the first response of the field was to doubt the measurement. Kepler, launched 2009, then found thousands by watching for the 0.01% dip as a planet crossed its star's face; TESS and the radial velocity surveys continue, and JWST is now reading the chemistry of their atmospheres.

  • Hot Jupiters — giants in days-long orbits; rare in truth, over-represented early because they are the easiest to see.
  • Super-Earths and sub-Neptunes — between Earth and Neptune in size, the most common kind of planet in the galaxy, and we have none.
  • Compact multiples — TRAPPIST-1: seven Earth-sized worlds round a red dwarf, all of them inside Mercury's orbit.
  • Rogue planets — thrown out of their systems, drifting unlit between the stars, and possibly outnumbering the bound ones.

Best current estimate: most stars have planets, and something like one in five Sun-like stars has a roughly Earth-sized planet in the temperate zone. The error bars on that figure are large and honest. ◆ speculative and contested.

On the habitable zone. It means only that liquid water could persist on a surface at that distance, given an atmosphere we have assumed. It is a crude first filter, not a verdict: Venus is in the Sun's zone. The order files "a planet in the habitable zone" as ◆ interesting, and "a planet where something lives" as ◆ unestablished, until a biosignature survives peer review. The contact question itself is held at Limb VII.

Third Subject

Galaxies, and What They Are Made Of

A hundred years ago it was an open question whether anything existed outside the Milky Way. The answer arrived in 1924 and was the largest single expansion of the known world in the history of the species.

Kinds ◆

Four Shapes and Their Habits

  • Spirals — a rotating disc of gas, dust and young blue stars with a bulge of old ones; arms are density waves, not solid structures, so stars drift through them like traffic through a jam. Still making stars.
  • Barred spirals — about two thirds of spirals, ours among them; the bar funnels gas inward and feeds the centre.
  • Ellipticals — from dwarfs to the monsters at cluster cores; little gas, little dust, almost no new stars, orbits pointing every which way. Built by merger.
  • Lenticulars and irregulars — the discs that have run out of gas, and the small torn things like the Magellanic Clouds.

Scale: a dwarf spheroidal may hold a thousand stars; IC 1101 holds perhaps a hundred trillion. "Galaxy" covers eleven orders of magnitude of mass.

The Active Ones ◆

Quasars, Seyferts, Blazars, Radio Galaxies

In a few per cent of galaxies the central black hole is eating, and the infalling matter outshines every star in the host combined. These were catalogued as four different kinds of object before it was realised they are mostly the same object seen from different angles — the unification model: looking down the jet gives a blazar, looking across the torus gives a Seyfert 2, looking past it gives a quasar.

3C 273, the first quasar recognised as such (Schmidt, 1963), sits 2.4 billion light years away and is still magnitude 12.9. Because they are visible across the universe, quasars are how the early universe is surveyed at all.

The Missing Mass ◆

Rotation Curves, and the Argument They Started

Vera Rubin and Kent Ford, through the 1970s, measured how fast the outskirts of spiral galaxies turn. By Kepler the speed should fall off with distance from the mass. It does not: it flattens. Either there is five times more mass than we can see, arranged in a halo, or gravity itself is not what we think at low accelerations.

The evidence has gone heavily one way. Gravitational lensing, the acoustic peaks in the cosmic microwave background, and above all the Bullet Cluster — where the lensing mass walked straight through the collision while the visible gas stalled — are hard to answer with modified gravity. But no dark matter particle has been detected in sixty years of looking, and that is not nothing. ◆ the observations. ◆ speculative and contested — what the missing mass is.

The Larger Pattern ◆

Groups, Clusters, Filaments, Voids

Galaxies are not scattered evenly. They sit in groups of dozens and clusters of thousands; the clusters sit on filaments of dark matter and hot gas; the filaments wall off voids hundreds of millions of light years across with almost nothing in them. The whole is called the cosmic web, and it grew from density ripples of one part in a hundred thousand, which are visible, directly, in the microwave background.

Our address: the Milky Way, in the Local Group, falling towards the Virgo Cluster, in the Laniakea Supercluster — defined in 2014 by Tully and colleagues not by where things are but by which way they are flowing.

Fourth Subject

The Four Classes of Black Hole

Not a hole and not made of anything. A region where the escape velocity exceeds the speed of light, which is to say a region whose future points inward.

Class I ◆

Stellar-Mass — 3 to 150 Suns

The remnant of a massive star whose core could not be held up. Cygnus X-1, identified in 1971, was the first that convinced people — and the subject of a bet Hawking placed against himself and lost in 1990. A handful of dozens are known in our galaxy by their X-rays; a hundred million are expected to exist here, almost all of them dark and undetectable.

Since 2015 they are also heard: LIGO's detection of GW150914, two holes of 36 and 29 solar masses merging 1.3 billion light years away, released three solar masses as pure spacetime ripple in a fifth of a second. Over two hundred mergers are now catalogued.

Class II ◆

Intermediate — 100 to 100,000 Suns

The awkward middle, long suspected and poorly evidenced. Candidates appear as ultraluminous X-ray sources in other galaxies and at the centres of some globular clusters, and GW190521 — a merger producing a 142-solar-mass remnant — is the cleanest case yet, because that mass falls in the range core collapse is not supposed to produce.

Why it matters: if the supermassive holes grew from stellar ones, this is the rung they climbed. If there is no rung, they had to start large. ◆ speculative and contested — real objects, uncertain population.

Class III ◆

Supermassive — Millions to Tens of Billions

One at the centre of essentially every large galaxy. Sagittarius A* is 4.3 million solar masses, a fact established by watching individual stars orbit an invisible point for thirty years — Ghez and Genzel, Nobel 2020. S2 passes within 120 AU at 3% of light speed, and its orbit precesses by exactly the amount general relativity demands.

M87* is 6.5 billion solar masses, larger across than Neptune's orbit, and was the first ever photographed. The galaxy-black hole mass relation (M–σ) is tight enough that the two must have grown together — ◆ how, exactly, is unsettled, and JWST finding quasars already a billion suns heavy at 700 million years after the Big Bang has made it sharply worse.

Class IV ◆

Primordial — Hypothetical

Hawking and Zel'dovich proposed that density fluctuations in the first second could have collapsed directly into holes of any mass at all, from a mountain to a star. None has been observed. Most of the mass window has been closed by microlensing surveys and by the absence of the gamma-ray burst a small one would give as it finished evaporating.

A narrow window remains open, which is why they are still discussed as a dark matter candidate. ◆ speculative and contested — a respectable hypothesis with no detection behind it.

Evaporation, and the Information Question

Hawking, 1974: quantum field theory near a horizon makes a black hole glow, faintly, at a temperature inversely proportional to its mass. A solar-mass hole radiates at 60 nanokelvin — colder than the microwave background, so in this era it absorbs more than it emits and grows. Left in an empty universe it would take 1067 years to evaporate. ◆ established theory; never observed, and not plausibly observable.

The paradox that follows is the hardest open problem in physics: if the hole evaporates into thermal radiation, the information that fell in is destroyed, and quantum mechanics forbids that. The current best answer — that the entropy of the radiation follows a curve that turns over (the Page curve), recovered since 2019 from the "island" calculations — is genuine progress and is not a resolution. ◆ speculative and contested. The order's Limb XI·i takes up what this means for identity; nothing on this page licenses that reading.

How Any of This Is Known

Every distance in this page rests on a ladder, and each rung is calibrated by the one below it. Kick out a rung and everything above it moves.

  1. Radar and spacecraft fix the astronomical unit to the metre.
  2. Parallax — the shift of a nearby star against the far ones as the Earth crosses its orbit. Bessel got 61 Cygni in 1838; Gaia has now done it for nearly two billion stars.
  3. Spectroscopic and cluster fitting — put a star on the main sequence, compare apparent with absolute brightness.
  4. Cepheids and RR Lyrae — period gives luminosity, out to about 100 million light years.
  5. Type Ia supernovae — standard candles to billions.
  6. Redshift — and Hubble's law, for everything beyond.

There is an open crack in it. The expansion rate measured locally up the ladder (~73 km/s/Mpc) disagrees with the rate inferred from the microwave background (~67) by far more than either side's error bars allow. This is the Hubble tension, it has not gone away with better data, and it is the most likely place for the next real surprise. ◆ the measurements. ◆ the explanation.

The Ledger

What Holds Up, and What Does Not

Survives

  • ◆ Stellar nucleosynthesis. The heavy elements in your body were assembled in stars and in neutron-star collisions. Measured in spectra, in meteorites and in the 2017 kilonova GW170817, which made gold in front of us.
  • ◆ The expansion of space, and its acceleration. Redshift, supernovae, the microwave background and baryon acoustic oscillations all agree.
  • ◆ Black holes as real objects. Orbits, X-ray binaries, gravitational waves and two photographs.
  • ◆ Planets are ordinary. Nearly six thousand confirmed; most stars have them.
  • ◆ Ancient astronomy was real astronomy. Babylonian ephemerides, the Antikythera mechanism, Mayan Venus tables, Newgrange and Stonehenge aligned to the solstices. Precise, predictive, and done without a telescope.

Does Not Survive

  • ◆ "Astronomers have found a planet exactly like Earth." They have not. They have found planets of similar radius in similar orbits, with unknown atmospheres.
  • ◆ Planetary alignments causing anything on Earth. The tidal pull of Jupiter at closest approach is smaller than that of the building you are in.
  • ◆ The dark side of the Moon. There is a far side. Every part of it gets sunlight; it is tidal locking, not shadow.
  • ◆ Stars that "are already dead". Almost every naked-eye star is within a few thousand light years and alive. The line is poetry, not astronomy.
  • ◆ Nibiru, Planet X as catastrophe. A genuine ninth-planet hypothesis exists for a distant body perturbing Kuiper belt orbits — and it is an ordinary, cold, far-off world, not an arrival.

The Words

Light year
The distance light travels in a year: 9.46 trillion km. A distance, never a time.
Parsec
3.26 light years — the distance at which one astronomical unit subtends one arcsecond. The professional's unit, because it falls straight out of a parallax measurement.
Magnitude
Brightness on a backwards logarithmic scale inherited from Hipparchus: smaller is brighter, five magnitudes is a factor of a hundred. Apparent is how bright it looks; absolute is how bright it would look at 10 parsecs.
Metallicity
To an astronomer, every element heavier than helium is a metal. A star's metallicity tells you which generation it belongs to.
Schwarzschild radius
The horizon radius of a non-rotating black hole: about 3 km per solar mass. The Earth's would be 9 mm.
Redshift (z)
The stretching of light by the expansion of space. z = 1 means the universe has doubled in size since that light set out.
Standard candle
An object whose true brightness is known, so its distance follows from how faint it looks. Cepheids and type Ia supernovae.
Accretion disc
Matter spiralling in, shedding angular momentum, and heating by friction until it shines — the most efficient energy source known, up to 40% of rest mass against hydrogen fusion's 0.7%.

A Closing Stance

The sky is the one place where the order's rule on evidence costs nothing and gains everything. There is no need to embellish a universe that contains a star spinning 716 times a second, a hole the size of a solar system photographed by a telescope the size of the Earth, and a number of galaxies that nobody can write down honestly without saying "about".

Wonder does not require the claims to be loose. It requires looking up often, and for long enough that the slow things become visible. Test everything kindly.
