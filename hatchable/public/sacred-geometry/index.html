Limb III

Sacred Geometry

Where the pattern is real, where the mathematics is beautiful, and where the claims quietly stop being true. Twelve plates, four closed classification theorems, the mechanism behind the golden angle, the minuted arguments of medieval masons, and a dated account of where the modern genre actually came from.

Geometry earned the adjective sacred honestly. For most of recorded history it was the one place where a person could meet something that was certainly true, could not have been otherwise, and had evidently not been invented by anybody. Numbers can be argued with. A proof cannot. That is as close to a religious experience as reasoning gets, and the Pythagoreans, Plato, the cathedral masons and the Islamic tilers all responded in the same way — by treating form as a doorway rather than a decoration.

They were not being superstitious. They had noticed something that remains genuinely strange: the same small set of shapes keeps arriving, unbidden, in systems that have no contact with one another. Hexagons in honeycomb and in cooling basalt. Logarithmic spirals in nautilus shells and in galaxies. The same seventeen symmetry groups in Moorish tilework and in crystal diffraction. Something about the constraints of space itself keeps producing the same answers, and the question of why is open.

This order keeps that reverence and adds a ledger. Some of what circulates under the name is exact, provable and astonishing. Some of it is measurement-shopping: choose your lines, ignore the misses, publish the hit. We mark the difference on every plate, because a tradition that cannot survive a tape measure does not need defending. It needs editing.

I · The Five Solids, and the Shape of a Real Proof

◆ The classification of the regular convex polyhedra is the oldest complete classification theorem in mathematics, and the argument costs two sentences. Each face must be a regular p-gon; q of them meet at each vertex; the vertex angle of a regular p-gon is 180°(p−2)/p; and for a corner to fold up rather than lie flat you need q · 180°(p−2)/p < 360°, which rearranges to 1/p + 1/q > 1/2. The integer solutions with p, q ≥ 3 are exactly {3,3}, {3,4}, {4,3}, {3,5} and {5,3} — the Schläfli symbols of the tetrahedron, octahedron, cube, icosahedron and dodecahedron. There is nothing to discover and nothing to believe. The list is forced.

◆ It gets better, and this is the part the mystical literature almost never reaches. Ask the same question in four dimensions and you get six regular polytopes, including the 24-cell, which has no analogue in any other dimension, and the 600-cell with its 120 vertices. Ask in five dimensions or any dimension above, and you get three, for ever: the simplex, the hypercube and the cross-polytope. ◆ Reality is at its most ornamented in three and four dimensions and austere everywhere else. If you want a genuinely numinous fact about the structure of space, that is one, and it needs no decoration.

◆ On the archaeology: the carved stone balls of Neolithic Scotland, several hundred of them from around 3000 BCE, are regularly offered as evidence that the five solids were known four millennia before Plato. They are not. The great majority have six knobs, the symmetry is approximate, no icosahedral example survives scrutiny, and the Ashmolean's famous “Platonic set” photograph is of objects whose geometry was tidied in the drawing. The balls are beautiful and genuinely puzzling; what they are not is a proof of lost knowledge.

◆ Plato's assignment in the Timaeus — tetrahedron to fire, cube to earth, octahedron to air, icosahedron to water, dodecahedron to “the whole” — is wrong as physics and precise as method. He gives reasons: fire is sharp, so the sharpest solid; earth is stable, so the one that stacks. That is a falsifiable theory of matter built from symmetry, written in the fourth century BCE. It failed, and the way of thinking survived: the modern classification of fundamental particles by their representations of symmetry groups is the same move, done with better mathematics.

II · φ: What Is True, What Is Measured, What Is Sold

◆ φ = (1+√5)/2 = 1.6180339887…, the unique positive root of x² = x + 1, hence the only number that is its own reciprocal plus one. It has the slowest continued fraction of any irrational, [1;1,1,1,…], which is what “most irrational” means technically: by Hurwitz's theorem of 1891, no number is worse approximated by rationals. Its convergents are the Fibonacci ratios 1/1, 2/1, 3/2, 5/3, 8/5, 13/8, 21/13 — which is why Fibonacci numbers and φ are the same fact told twice.

◆ The biology is now largely solved and is more interesting than the mysticism it displaced. Hofmeister's rule (1868) observes that each new primordium forms in the largest available gap. Vogel's model (1979) showed that a fixed divergence of 137.5° with radial spacing proportional to √n reproduces sunflower heads exactly. Douady and Couder (1992) got Fibonacci spirals out of ferrofluid droplets falling into a dish of silicone oil in a magnetic field — no genes, no cells, no evolution, just mutual repulsion and a periodic source. And Reinhardt and colleagues (Nature, 2003) identified the mechanism in the plant: the auxin efflux carrier PIN1 pumps the growth hormone towards existing primordia, locally depleting the field around them, so the next bud can only form where the depletion is weakest. Golden angle as an emergent consequence of a pump.

◆ Equally important, and almost never printed: it does not always work. Roughly 10 per cent of sunflower heads show Lucas numbers (1, 3, 4, 7, 11, 18) rather than Fibonacci; some show double Fibonacci; a few are simply irregular. The 2016 Royal Society Open Science citizen-science count of 657 sunflowers — the largest ever made — found strong Fibonacci dominance and a real tail of non-Fibonacci and disordered heads. Reporting the tail is what makes the dominance meaningful.

The claims that fail

  • ◆ The Parthenon. There is no φ in the surviving building specification, and whether a golden rectangle appears depends entirely on which stylobate step you start from and whether you include the now-missing pediment. George Markowsky's Misconceptions about the Golden Ratio (College Mathematics Journal, 1992) works through the measurements; the fit requires choices made after the conclusion.
  • ◆ The Great Pyramid. The slope does give a ratio near φ, and also one near 4/π, and also 14/11 in the Egyptians' own seked units of palms per cubit — which is the one unit the builders actually used and the one that requires no irrational number at all. Occam has a view.
  • ◆ The nautilus. A genuine logarithmic spiral, with a growth ratio of roughly 1.33 per turn, not 1.618. Measured repeatedly. Still reprinted weekly.
  • ◆ Leonardo's φ. He illustrated Pacioli's De Divina Proportione (1509) with the polyhedra and never used the ratio as a compositional rule in any painting; the Vitruvian Man is built on Vitruvius's own stated whole-number fractions.
  • ◆ The “most beautiful rectangle”. Fechner's 1876 preference experiment is the usual citation. Later replications with better controls — Godkewitsch 1974 onward — find the preference is small, unstable, and largely an artefact of the range of rectangles offered.

◆ The order's position: φ is a genuinely deep number whose actual depth is combinatorial and dynamical, and the folklore around it is almost entirely nineteenth-century invention attached to ancient buildings that cannot answer back. Keeping the real fact and discarding the glamour makes the subject more interesting, not less. A pump in a shoot tip performing an optimisation is a better story than a secret.

III · Symmetry, and the Theorems That Closed It

◆ There are exactly seventeen ways to repeat a pattern across a plane. Not seventeen that anyone has found — seventeen that can exist. The result was proved by Evgraf Fedorov in 1891 and independently by Pólya in 1924, and it classifies every wallpaper, every tiled floor and every textile print ever made or makeable. In three dimensions the same question gives the 230 crystallographic space groups, derived independently by Fedorov, Schoenflies and Barlow between 1891 and 1894, and every crystal structure ever solved belongs to one of them. On a two-dimensional surface with a frieze you get seven groups, and on the sphere the finite point groups. The whole of ornament is a finite list.

◆ The famous claim that the Alhambra contains all seventeen is contested and probably false. Edith Müller's 1944 thesis counted eleven; later authors have argued for thirteen, fifteen or all seventeen depending on how generously one treats colour, damage and later restoration. Branko Grünbaum's “What Symmetry Groups Are Present in the Alhambra?” (Notices of the AMS, 2006) is the sober account. ◆ That the real number is thirteen-ish rather than seventeen does not diminish the Nasrid craftsmen at all; it means they explored most of a space whose boundaries nobody would describe for another five hundred years.

◆ Girih. Peter Lu and Paul Steinhardt showed in Science (2007) that by the fifteenth century Islamic designers were working not with compass-and-straightedge construction lines but with a set of five decorated tiles — the girih tiles — and that the Darb-i Imam shrine in Isfahan (1453) carries a near-perfect quasi-periodic pattern with Penrose-like properties, five centuries early. The Topkapı Scroll, a 29-metre design manual of around 1500, preserves the working method. The claim is carefully limited and all the stronger for it: the designers had the tiles and the pattern, not the theory of aperiodicity.

◆ And the deepest true version of “geometry governs reality” is not mystical at all. Noether's theorem (Emmy Noether, 1918) proves that every continuous symmetry of a physical system corresponds to a conserved quantity: symmetry under time-translation gives conservation of energy, under spatial translation gives momentum, under rotation gives angular momentum. The conservation laws are consequences of symmetry. That is a far larger claim than anything in the sacred-geometry literature, it is proved, and it is why this limb exists.

IV · Why Nature Keeps Arriving at the Same Shapes

◆ The honest answer is not resonance or blueprint. It is that a small number of physical constraints act everywhere, and they have few solutions.

  • ◆ Minimisation. Surface tension minimises area; elastic sheets minimise bending energy; soap films solve in milliseconds a problem that is computationally hard. Hexagons win in foam because the honeycomb conjecture is true (Hales, 1999).
  • ◆ Packing. Thue proved hexagonal packing optimal in the plane in 1890; Hales proved Kepler's three-dimensional conjecture in 1998, with the formal verification (“Flyspeck”) completed by machine in 2014 after referees said they were 99 per cent certain and could not finish checking.
  • ◆ Reaction–diffusion. Turing's 1952 paper The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis showed two chemicals — a slow-spreading activator and a fast-spreading inhibitor — spontaneously produce spots, stripes and whorls from a uniform start. Kondo and Asai confirmed it in zebrafish stripes in Nature (1995), watching the pattern reorganise as the fish grew exactly as the equations predicted; Sheth and colleagues demonstrated a Turing mechanism setting mammalian digit number in Science (2012).
  • ◆ Branching and flow. Murray's law (1926) predicts that a vessel's radius cubed equals the sum of its daughters' radii cubed, minimising the cost of pumping, and it holds in arteries, in xylem and in insect tracheae.
  • ◆ Fracture and contraction. The hexagonal columns of the Giant's Causeway and of Devils Tower are cooling cracks: a contracting front cracks at 120° junctions for the same energy reason the foam does. Goehring and Morris reproduced them in drying cornflour slurry.
  • ◆ Scaling and self-similarity. Anything that grows by adding proportionally to what it already has traces a logarithmic spiral — shells, horns, claws, cyclones, spiral galaxies. The spiral is not a signature. It is what multiplication looks like drawn over time.

◆ What survives of the old intuition, then, is this: form really is law made visible, but the law is boundary conditions and energy minimisation rather than intention. That is a smaller metaphysical claim and a far larger scientific one, and the order would rather have the larger of the two.

V · The Documentary Record, and Where the Modern Genre Came From

◆ The strongest historical evidence that builders thought geometrically is not mystical, it is administrative. In 1392 the chapter of Milan Cathedral held a formal dispute, minuted and surviving, over whether the building should be raised ad quadratum — proportions from the square — or ad triangulum, from the equilateral triangle. The French consultant Jean Mignot told the Italians ars sine scientia nihil est, art without knowledge is nothing. They proceeded, awkwardly, on a modified triangle. That single document tells you more about medieval design practice than every speculative cathedral-geometry book combined.

◆ Likewise Villard de Honnecourt's portfolio (c. 1230), with its constructions for laying out a cloister and splitting a stone; Roriczer's Büchlein von der Fialen Gerechtigkeit (1486), which teaches the rotated-square method for a pinnacle; and Lon Shelby's scholarship showing that masons worked with constructive geometry — ruler, cord and template — rather than the arithmetic they mostly could not do. The “secret of the masons” was real, was a trade secret, and consisted of procedures rather than revelations.

◆ Further back: the Rhind Papyrus (c. 1650 BCE, copying a text two centuries older) computes a circle's area as (8d/9)², implying π ≈ 3.1605 — an error of 0.6 per cent. The Babylonian tablet YBC 7289 gives √2 as 1;24,51,10 in base 60, correct to six decimal places, around 1800 BCE. Plimpton 322 lists Pythagorean triples a thousand years before Pythagoras, though Mansfield and Wildberger's 2017 claim that it is a trigonometric table remains disputed. The Śulba Sūtras (c. 800–500 BCE) give altar constructions and an excellent √2 approximation. This is real mathematics, done for altars, fields and taxes, and it needs no embellishment.

◆ Alexander Thom's “megalithic yard” of 2.72 feet, proposed in the 1950s–70s from surveys of hundreds of stone circles, is the best-argued case for a prehistoric standard unit — and the statistical reanalyses by David Kendall and P. R. Freeman in the 1970s found the evidence consistent with builders pacing out lengths with no common unit at all. Thom's surveys remain valuable; his conclusion does not survive the statistics. ◆ We include this because it is the model case: careful fieldwork, an attractive hypothesis, and a null result that the popular literature simply never absorbed.

The genre is younger than it looks

◆ “Sacred geometry” as a shelf in a bookshop is a twentieth-century product. R. A. Schwaller de Lubicz spent fifteen years at Luxor and published Le Temple de l'Homme (1957), arguing the temple encodes the proportions of the human body; Egyptology has not accepted it. Robert Lawlor, his translator, wrote the Thames & Hudson Sacred Geometry (1982) that defined the modern field. Drunvalo Melchizedek's Flower of Life workshops and books (1990s) added Metatron's Cube as the alleged source of the five solids and the “merkaba” star tetrahedron.

◆ Two dating facts that the genre depends on and that do not hold. First, the Flower of Life figures on the granite of the Osireion at Abydos are not carved: they are drawn in red ochre, and the accompanying Greek inscriptions date the graffiti to the Ptolemaic or Roman period — roughly 2,000 years after the structure was built, and almost certainly the work of visitors, not builders. Second, Metatron's Cube takes its name from the angel of the Hebrew Merkabah literature and appears as a figure in the modern occult revival; the Platonic solids can be projected into its thirteen-circle scaffold, which is a drawing exercise, not a derivation. They were classified by angle defect two thousand years earlier and owe it nothing.

◆ We say all this with affection. The genre got one thing exactly right — that form is worth staring at until it tells you something — and then dated its own evidence wrongly. The correction costs nothing that matters.

VI · How to Test a Geometric Claim

Six questions. Any claim on this page, or anyone else's, should survive all six before it is believed. Most of the famous ones fail at the second.

  • ◆ Which landmarks, chosen when? Name the two points before measuring. A building has thousands of candidate pairs; with enough pairs, every ratio you like is present somewhere.
  • ◆ What precision, and what error bar? “1.6” is not φ; it is also 8/5, and π/2 is 1.5708, and √e is 1.6487. Claimed matches to two significant figures are claims about nothing.
  • ◆ How many misses? Publish the ratios that did not work. A hit rate is meaningless without the denominator.
  • ◆ What units did the builders use? A result that appears in cubits, sekeds or paces and vanishes in metres is usually an artefact of the metre.
  • ◆ Is there a mechanism? Phyllotaxis has one (auxin transport). Pyramid-φ has none proposed that the builders could have executed.
  • ◆ Could it come out otherwise? If no possible measurement would have refuted the claim, the claim is not about the building.

Apply these to our own plates. Plates IX, X, XI and XII pass because they are theorems or reproducible experiments. The claim that the Osireion carvings are ancient fails at the fourth and fifth. We would rather you caught us than took us at our word.

What Survives the Tape Measure

  • ◆ The five regular solids — and the proof that there are only five.
  • ◆ Hexagonal close-packing in foam, honeycomb, basalt and graphene.
  • ◆ Phyllotaxis and the golden angle, reproduced in non-living systems.
  • ◆ Minimal surfaces: soap films solving an optimisation problem instantly.
  • ◆ Logarithmic spirals in shells, horns and galaxies, from simple growth rules.
  • ◆ The seventeen wallpaper groups, and the 230 crystallographic groups in three dimensions.
  • ◆ Penrose tilings and quasicrystals — the "impossible" five-fold symmetry, found in matter.
  • ◆ Voronoi partitioning in giraffe hide, dragonfly wings and dried mud.
  • ◆ Turing patterns: two diffusing chemicals producing spots, stripes and whorls.
  • ◆ Only five regular solids in three dimensions, six in four, three in every dimension above. Forced, not chosen.
  • ◆ Exactly seventeen wallpaper groups and 230 space groups — complete classifications, not surveys.
  • ◆ φ as the most irrational number, by Hurwitz's theorem, and the reason 137.5° packs best.
  • ◆ Plateau's laws: films at 120°, edges at 109.47°. Observed 1873, proved by Jean Taylor in 1976.
  • ◆ The honeycomb conjecture (Hales 1999) and the Kepler conjecture (Hales 1998, machine-verified 2014).
  • ◆ Murray's law for branching vessels, holding in arteries, xylem and tracheae alike.
  • ◆ Noether's theorem: every continuous symmetry yields a conservation law. The real version of "geometry is the law".
  • ◆ Girih tiles at Darb-i Imam, 1453 — quasi-periodic design five centuries before the mathematics (Lu & Steinhardt, 2007).
  • ◆ The Milan Cathedral dispute of 1392, minuted: ad quadratum against ad triangulum, in the builders' own words.

What Does Not

  • ◆ φ in the Great Pyramid and the Parthenon — selective measurement, retrofitted.
  • ◆ Geometry as a carrier for healing, intention or water memory.
  • ◆ "Sacred" frequencies derived by numerology from shape — 432 Hz included.
  • ◆ Metatron's Cube as the source "from which the five solids derive". They can be projected into it, which is a weaker and much later claim.
  • ◆ The Fibonacci sequence as a universal constant of beauty. Count the failures as well as the pine cones.
  • ◆ The Flower of Life at the Osireion as pharaonic. It is red-ochre graffiti with Greek inscriptions beside it, Ptolemaic or Roman.
  • ◆ The Neolithic carved stone balls as a set of Platonic solids. Mostly six-knobbed, approximate, and no icosahedron survives inspection.
  • ◆ Thom's megalithic yard as a proven prehistoric standard. Kendall and Freeman's reanalyses find pacing, not a unit.
  • ◆ The golden rectangle as the most pleasing shape. Fechner 1876 does not replicate cleanly.
  • ◆ All seventeen wallpaper groups at the Alhambra. The careful counts give eleven to thirteen, which is still extraordinary.
  • ◆ Leonardo composing with φ. He drew Pacioli's polyhedra and built the Vitruvian Man on Vitruvius's whole-number fractions.
  • ◆ Any argument whose final step is "…and therefore consciousness".

None of this is shameful, and none of it is banned here. It is myth, and myth is a legitimate shelf in this library — it simply is not measurement, and must never be sold as any.

A Short History of the Idea

  • c. 3000 BCE Megalithic builders align Newgrange and Maeshowe to the solstice sun with an accuracy that still works.
  • c. 1650 BCE The Rhind Papyrus: Egyptian scribes computing areas and approximating π as 256/81.
  • c. 1800 BCE Babylonian tablet YBC 7289 records √2 as 1;24,51,10 — correct to six decimal places — and Plimpton 322 lists Pythagorean triples.
  • c. 800–500 BCE The Śulba Sūtras set out altar geometry in India, with their own excellent √2 approximation.
  • c. 530 BCE Pythagoras and his school make number the substance of reality — and, the story goes, meet irrationality and hate it.
  • c. 360 BCE Plato's Timaeus assigns the five solids to the elements. The assignment is wrong; the question behind it is excellent.
  • c. 300 BCE Euclid's Elements, ending at the five solids. Still the model for what a proof is.
  • c. 300 CE Pappus of Alexandria argues the bee's hexagon is the most economical cell. It will take seventeen centuries to prove him right.
  • c. 1230 Villard de Honnecourt's portfolio records the masons' constructive geometry — cord, template and rotated square.
  • 1392 The Milan Cathedral dispute: ad quadratum or ad triangulum, minuted, with Mignot's rebuke that art without knowledge is nothing.
  • 1453 The Darb-i Imam shrine at Isfahan is decorated with girih tiling that is very nearly quasi-periodic.
  • 1486 Roriczer prints the pinnacle construction — a trade secret, published.
  • 9th–15th c. Islamic geometers develop girih tiling to a sophistication Western mathematics would not formalise until the twentieth century.
  • 1202 Fibonacci's Liber Abaci introduces the sequence to Europe — as a problem about rabbits.
  • 1509 Pacioli's De Divina Proportione, illustrated by Leonardo, gives φ its lasting mystique.
  • 1596 Kepler nests the five solids inside the planetary orbits in Mysterium Cosmographicum. Beautiful, wholly wrong, and the work that led him to the right answer.
  • 1882 Lindemann proves π transcendental. Squaring the circle is dead.
  • 1619 Kepler's Harmonices Mundi: the solids abandoned, the third law found, the music of the spheres kept as a figure of speech.
  • 1868 Hofmeister's rule — each new bud forms in the largest gap — the seed of every later phyllotaxis model.
  • 1873 Plateau publishes the laws of soap films from pure observation, having been blind for thirty years.
  • 1890–91 Thue proves hexagonal packing optimal in the plane; Fedorov classifies the seventeen wallpaper groups; Hurwitz pins down what "most irrational" means.
  • 1891–94 The 230 space groups are derived three times independently, by Fedorov, Schoenflies and Barlow.
  • 1918 Emmy Noether proves that every continuous symmetry corresponds to a conserved quantity.
  • 1917 D'Arcy Thompson's On Growth and Form: biological shape as physics, not decoration.
  • 1952 Turing's reaction–diffusion paper explains how animals get their markings.
  • 1957 Schwaller de Lubicz publishes Le Temple de l'Homme; the modern "sacred geometry" genre begins, outside Egyptology.
  • 1970s Kendall and Freeman reanalyse Thom's stone-circle surveys: no megalithic yard required.
  • 1976 Jean Taylor proves Plateau's laws.
  • 1979–92 Vogel models the sunflower head; Douady and Couder grow Fibonacci spirals out of ferrofluid with no biology present.
  • 1982 Lawlor's Sacred Geometry defines the popular field.
  • 1992 Markowsky's Misconceptions about the Golden Ratio dismantles the Parthenon and the rest, politely.
  • 1995 Kondo and Asai watch zebrafish stripes rearrange exactly as Turing's equations predict.
  • 1998–2014 Hales proves the Kepler conjecture, then the honeycomb conjecture in 1999; the Flyspeck project completes a machine-checked proof.
  • 2003 Reinhardt and colleagues identify PIN1 auxin transport as the mechanism behind the golden angle.
  • 2007 Lu and Steinhardt publish the girih tile analysis in Science.
  • 2012 A Turing mechanism is shown to set the number of digits on a mammalian limb.
  • 1974 Penrose tiles the plane aperiodically with five-fold symmetry.
  • 1982–2011 Shechtman observes quasicrystals, is ridiculed, and is vindicated with a Nobel Prize.

Note the shape of the list. The mystical framings keep being wrong in detail and right to be curious — Kepler's nested solids are nonsense, and chasing them produced the laws of planetary motion. That is the most useful thing this limb has to teach.

Working Glossary

Vesica piscis
The lens formed by two circles each passing through the other's centre. Ratio √3.
φ (phi)
(1+√5)/2 ≈ 1.6180339887. The ratio where a : b = (a+b) : a.
Golden angle
360°/φ² ≈ 137.507°. The divergence angle that never repeats.
Phyllotaxis
The arrangement of leaves, seeds or scales around a growth axis.
Parastichy
One of the visible spiral families in a sunflower or cone. Counts are Fibonacci.
Platonic solid
A convex polyhedron with identical regular faces and identical vertices. There are five.
Dual
The solid obtained by putting a vertex at each face-centre. Cube ↔ octahedron; dodecahedron ↔ icosahedron; the tetrahedron is its own.
Aperiodic tiling
A tiling that never repeats by translation, yet has long-range order.
Wallpaper group
One of the 17 distinct symmetry structures a repeating plane pattern can have.
Voronoi cell
The region closer to one seed point than to any other. Nature's default partition.
Angle defect
360° minus the angles meeting at a vertex. Positive and the corner folds into a solid; zero and it lies flat.
Schläfli symbol
{p,q}: p-sided faces, q to a vertex. The five solids are {3,3}, {3,4}, {4,3}, {3,5}, {5,3}.
Continued fraction
A number written as a nest of reciprocals. φ is all ones, which is why it is the hardest number to approximate.
Crystallographic restriction
The theorem that a lattice admits only 2-, 3-, 4- and 6-fold rotation. Five-fold symmetry cannot be periodic.
Quasicrystal
Matter with long-range order and no periodicity. Predicted by tiling theory, found in 1982, Nobel in 2011.
Space group
One of the 230 symmetry types available to a three-dimensional crystal.
Reaction–diffusion
Turing's mechanism: a slow activator and a fast inhibitor generating stripes and spots from uniformity.
Plateau's laws
Soap films meet three to an edge at 120°, and edges meet four to a point at 109.47°.
Minimal surface
A surface of locally least area for its boundary. Mean curvature zero everywhere.
Seked
The Egyptian measure of slope, in palms of run per cubit of rise. The unit the pyramid builders actually used.
Ad quadratum / ad triangulum
Medieval design by proportions derived from the square, or from the equilateral triangle.
Girih
The five decorated tiles behind much Islamic strapwork, and the geometry they generate.
Noether's theorem
Every continuous symmetry of a physical system yields a conserved quantity.

Now Put the Page Down

Draw It Yourself

Reading about this is nearly useless. The knowledge is in the hand. Compass, straightedge, one hour — and every figure on this page becomes something you have made rather than something you have been shown.

1 · The vesica, from nothing

Draw a circle. Put the compass point anywhere on its rim and, without changing the radius, draw a second. The lens where they overlap is the vesica piscis. Now join the two centres and the two crossing points: you have an exact equilateral triangle, and you have measured no angle at all.

This is the first proposition of Euclid's Elements. You have just done, by hand, the thing mathematics opens with.

2 · The seed of life, and why six

Keep the radius fixed. Walk the compass point around the first circle's rim, drawing a new circle at each crossing. After exactly six you arrive back where you began — no gap, no overlap.

You have just proved that six equal circles fit around one, which is the reason bees, basalt, foam and graphene all end up hexagonal. Nobody told the bees.

3 · The golden section, without arithmetic

Draw a square. From the midpoint of its base, open the compass to the opposite top corner and swing an arc down to the extended base line. The rectangle you can now complete is φ — 1.618… — and you got there with a right triangle of sides 1 and ½, no mysticism required.

4 · The pentagram hidden in the pentagon

Construct a regular pentagon, then join every vertex to every non-adjacent vertex. The pentagram appears, and inside it a smaller pentagon, and so on without end. Every length in the figure is in golden ratio to the next.

This self-similarity is said to have embarrassed the Pythagoreans badly: it is one of the routes to proving that √5 — and so φ — is irrational, and irrationality was not supposed to exist.

5 · A cube from a vesica

From the vesica you can raise a perpendicular; from a perpendicular, a square; from a square drawn twice with offset, the isometric cube. Nothing in the chain requires a protractor. Medieval masons worked this way because rope and compass are reliable on a building site and arithmetic is not.

6 · Then go outside and count

Find a pine cone, a sunflower head, a pineapple. Count the spirals clockwise, then anticlockwise. You will get consecutive Fibonacci numbers — 8 and 13, 13 and 21, 21 and 34.

Do this once and the difference between knowing and having been told becomes permanent. That is the entire pedagogy of this order in one afternoon.
