Limb III

Sacred Geometry

Where the pattern is real, where the mathematics is beautiful, and where the claims quietly stop being true.

Geometry earned the adjective sacred honestly. For most of recorded history it was the one place where a person could meet something that was certainly true, could not have been otherwise, and had evidently not been invented by anybody. Numbers can be argued with. A proof cannot. That is as close to a religious experience as reasoning gets, and the Pythagoreans, Plato, the cathedral masons and the Islamic tilers all responded in the same way — by treating form as a doorway rather than a decoration.

They were not being superstitious. They had noticed something that remains genuinely strange: the same small set of shapes keeps arriving, unbidden, in systems that have no contact with one another. Hexagons in honeycomb and in cooling basalt. Logarithmic spirals in nautilus shells and in galaxies. The same seventeen symmetry groups in Moorish tilework and in crystal diffraction. Something about the constraints of space itself keeps producing the same answers, and the question of why is open.

This order keeps that reverence and adds a ledger. Some of what circulates under the name is exact, provable and astonishing. Some of it is measurement-shopping: choose your lines, ignore the misses, publish the hit. We mark the difference on every plate, because a tradition that cannot survive a tape measure does not need defending. It needs editing.

What Survives the Tape Measure

  • ◆ The five regular solids — and the proof that there are only five.
  • ◆ Hexagonal close-packing in foam, honeycomb, basalt and graphene.
  • ◆ Phyllotaxis and the golden angle, reproduced in non-living systems.
  • ◆ Minimal surfaces: soap films solving an optimisation problem instantly.
  • ◆ Logarithmic spirals in shells, horns and galaxies, from simple growth rules.
  • ◆ The seventeen wallpaper groups, and the 230 crystallographic groups in three dimensions.
  • ◆ Penrose tilings and quasicrystals — the "impossible" five-fold symmetry, found in matter.
  • ◆ Voronoi partitioning in giraffe hide, dragonfly wings and dried mud.
  • ◆ Turing patterns: two diffusing chemicals producing spots, stripes and whorls.

What Does Not

  • ◆ φ in the Great Pyramid and the Parthenon — selective measurement, retrofitted.
  • ◆ Geometry as a carrier for healing, intention or water memory.
  • ◆ "Sacred" frequencies derived by numerology from shape — 432 Hz included.
  • ◆ Metatron's Cube as the source "from which the five solids derive". They can be projected into it, which is a weaker and much later claim.
  • ◆ The Fibonacci sequence as a universal constant of beauty. Count the failures as well as the pine cones.
  • ◆ Any argument whose final step is "…and therefore consciousness".

None of this is shameful, and none of it is banned here. It is myth, and myth is a legitimate shelf in this library — it simply is not measurement, and must never be sold as any.

A Short History of the Idea

  • c. 3000 BCE Megalithic builders align Newgrange and Maeshowe to the solstice sun with an accuracy that still works.
  • c. 1650 BCE The Rhind Papyrus: Egyptian scribes computing areas and approximating π as 256/81.
  • c. 530 BCE Pythagoras and his school make number the substance of reality — and, the story goes, meet irrationality and hate it.
  • c. 360 BCE Plato's Timaeus assigns the five solids to the elements. The assignment is wrong; the question behind it is excellent.
  • c. 300 BCE Euclid's Elements, ending at the five solids. Still the model for what a proof is.
  • 9th–15th c. Islamic geometers develop girih tiling to a sophistication Western mathematics would not formalise until the twentieth century.
  • 1202 Fibonacci's Liber Abaci introduces the sequence to Europe — as a problem about rabbits.
  • 1509 Pacioli's De Divina Proportione, illustrated by Leonardo, gives φ its lasting mystique.
  • 1596 Kepler nests the five solids inside the planetary orbits in Mysterium Cosmographicum. Beautiful, wholly wrong, and the work that led him to the right answer.
  • 1882 Lindemann proves π transcendental. Squaring the circle is dead.
  • 1917 D'Arcy Thompson's On Growth and Form: biological shape as physics, not decoration.
  • 1952 Turing's reaction–diffusion paper explains how animals get their markings.
  • 1974 Penrose tiles the plane aperiodically with five-fold symmetry.
  • 1982–2011 Shechtman observes quasicrystals, is ridiculed, and is vindicated with a Nobel Prize.

Note the shape of the list. The mystical framings keep being wrong in detail and right to be curious — Kepler's nested solids are nonsense, and chasing them produced the laws of planetary motion. That is the most useful thing this limb has to teach.

Working Glossary

Vesica piscis
The lens formed by two circles each passing through the other's centre. Ratio √3.
φ (phi)
(1+√5)/2 ≈ 1.6180339887. The ratio where a : b = (a+b) : a.
Golden angle
360°/φ² ≈ 137.507°. The divergence angle that never repeats.
Phyllotaxis
The arrangement of leaves, seeds or scales around a growth axis.
Parastichy
One of the visible spiral families in a sunflower or cone. Counts are Fibonacci.
Platonic solid
A convex polyhedron with identical regular faces and identical vertices. There are five.
Dual
The solid obtained by putting a vertex at each face-centre. Cube ↔ octahedron; dodecahedron ↔ icosahedron; the tetrahedron is its own.
Aperiodic tiling
A tiling that never repeats by translation, yet has long-range order.
Wallpaper group
One of the 17 distinct symmetry structures a repeating plane pattern can have.
Voronoi cell
The region closer to one seed point than to any other. Nature's default partition.

Now Put the Page Down

Draw It Yourself

Reading about this is nearly useless. The knowledge is in the hand. Compass, straightedge, one hour — and every figure on this page becomes something you have made rather than something you have been shown.

1 · The vesica, from nothing

Draw a circle. Put the compass point anywhere on its rim and, without changing the radius, draw a second. The lens where they overlap is the vesica piscis. Now join the two centres and the two crossing points: you have an exact equilateral triangle, and you have measured no angle at all.

This is the first proposition of Euclid's Elements. You have just done, by hand, the thing mathematics opens with.

2 · The seed of life, and why six

Keep the radius fixed. Walk the compass point around the first circle's rim, drawing a new circle at each crossing. After exactly six you arrive back where you began — no gap, no overlap.

You have just proved that six equal circles fit around one, which is the reason bees, basalt, foam and graphene all end up hexagonal. Nobody told the bees.

3 · The golden section, without arithmetic

Draw a square. From the midpoint of its base, open the compass to the opposite top corner and swing an arc down to the extended base line. The rectangle you can now complete is φ — 1.618… — and you got there with a right triangle of sides 1 and ½, no mysticism required.

4 · The pentagram hidden in the pentagon

Construct a regular pentagon, then join every vertex to every non-adjacent vertex. The pentagram appears, and inside it a smaller pentagon, and so on without end. Every length in the figure is in golden ratio to the next.

This self-similarity is said to have embarrassed the Pythagoreans badly: it is one of the routes to proving that √5 — and so φ — is irrational, and irrationality was not supposed to exist.

5 · A cube from a vesica

From the vesica you can raise a perpendicular; from a perpendicular, a square; from a square drawn twice with offset, the isometric cube. Nothing in the chain requires a protractor. Medieval masons worked this way because rope and compass are reliable on a building site and arithmetic is not.

6 · Then go outside and count

Find a pine cone, a sunflower head, a pineapple. Count the spirals clockwise, then anticlockwise. You will get consecutive Fibonacci numbers — 8 and 13, 13 and 21, 21 and 34.

Do this once and the difference between knowing and having been told becomes permanent. That is the entire pedagogy of this order in one afternoon.
