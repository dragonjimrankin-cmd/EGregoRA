Limb II

The Druids

A learned class that trained for twenty years, held a society's law, medicine, genealogy and calendar in living memory, and refused on principle to write any of it down. We are reconstructing them from the accounts of the army that destroyed them.

Begin Here

The Evidence Problem, Stated Plainly

Everything on this page rests on three kinds of source, and all three are compromised in different ways. Knowing how is the difference between study and fantasy.

Greek and Roman writers — Caesar, Strabo, Pliny, Tacitus, Diodorus. Caesar is the fullest and he was conquering Gaul while writing; a barbarian priesthood practising human sacrifice is precisely the justification an invading general requires. Pliny writes a century after the fact. Several of them are copying each other from a lost common source, which means four accounts may be one.

Irish and Welsh literature — the Táin, the Mabinogion, the law tracts. Rich, marvellous, and written down by Christian monks between the eighth and fourteenth centuries, from a society never conquered by Rome but thoroughly converted. The druids in them are often literary villains for a saint to defeat.

Archaeology — the only source with no agenda, and the one that cannot tell you what anybody believed. It gives us bog bodies, ritual deposits, temple enclosures, the Coligny calendar. It never gives us a word.

Grading throughout: ◆ established science · ◆ scholarship and history · ◆ speculative and contested · ◆ myth, tradition and primary esoteric text.

✦

The Order

Who They Actually Were

Not a priesthood in the modern sense. A learned class — closer to a combined judiciary, university, medical college and civil service, with the sacred woven through all of it.

◆

The Druid

The senior grade. Judge, arbitrator, theologian, astronomer, teacher. Caesar says they settled disputes both public and private, and that their most serious penalty was exclusion from sacrifice — effectively outlawry, since no one would then transact with you. A legal system enforced by the withdrawal of community rather than by force.

◆

The Bard

Keeper of memory and maker of reputation — genealogy, praise and, crucially, satire. Irish sources take bardic satire with complete seriousness as a weapon: a bad satire could raise blisters on a king's face and cost him his kingship, because a ruler who had lost face had lost standing. Reputation was power, and the bards held the instrument.

◆

The Vates

Diviner and natural philosopher — the fáith or ovate. Concerned with omens, sacrifice, healing and the reading of natural signs. Strabo distinguishes them from the druids proper, though the boundary is unclear and may be a Greek tidiness imposed on something messier.

◆

Who Could Join

Recruitment appears to have been from the aristocracy, and the class was exempt from both taxation and military service — an exemption Caesar notes attracted many applicants. This was a privileged estate, not a wandering mystic's calling.

◆

Were There Women?

Probably, though the direct evidence is thin. Roman sources mention Gallic female seers (dryades) in the third century CE; Irish literature has figures like Bodhmall and Fedelm with clearly druidic functions. Whether women held the senior judicial grade is unknown, and the confident modern claim that they did is reconstruction, not record.

◆

The Archdruid

Caesar reports a single chief druid over all Gaul, elected or occasionally fought over, and an annual assembly in the territory of the Carnutes — the centre of Gaul — where disputes from across the land were judged. If accurate, this is a pan-tribal institution operating above the tribes, which is politically remarkable.

✦

Twenty Years

The Training, and Why Nothing Was Written

Caesar: some remain in training for twenty years, and they commit a great number of verses to memory. They consider it improper to entrust their studies to writing — though they use Greek letters for almost all other business.

Read that last clause carefully, because it demolishes the romantic version. These were not people without literacy. They had access to the Greek alphabet and used it for accounts and correspondence. The refusal was specific and deliberate, and Caesar gives two reasons he was evidently told: so the teaching would not reach the common people, and so that students would not rely on the written word and let memory decay.

The second reason is better than it sounds. Oral cultures achieve feats of memory that literate cultures simply cannot — Vedic transmission preserved Sanskrit phonetically across three millennia, and Homeric performers held tens of thousands of lines. Metre, formula, melody and place are the machinery, and a trained memory is not a worse archive than a book. It is a different one, with the advantage of being inside a person and the catastrophic disadvantage of dying with them.

Which is exactly what happened. ◆ The curriculum — twenty years of law, lineage, astronomy, medicine, theology and verse — is gone absolutely, and no amount of modern reconstruction recovers it. When we say the druids are lost, this is the specific thing that was lost.

✦

The Ordinary Hours

How They Actually Lived

Strip the robes and the mist. This is the archaeology, which is the one source that cannot be lying to us about the daily texture of the thing.

Place ◆

Roundhouses, Not Stone Circles

Iron Age Britain and Gaul lived in timber roundhouses — a central hearth, a conical thatched roof, no chimney, the smoke filtering through the thatch and preserving it. Warm, dark, smoky, and acoustically extraordinary. Settlements ranged from single farmsteads to hillforts of several thousand people.

Note firmly: the druids did not build Stonehenge. It predates them by roughly two thousand years. The association is an eighteenth-century guess by William Stukeley that never let go.

Food ◆

What Was on the Table

Emmer and spelt wheat, barley — much of it as porridge, flatbread and beer. Cattle as the standard of wealth, sheep for wool, pigs for feasting. Dairy heavily. Hazelnuts, apples, blackberries, sloes. Isotope analysis of skeletons shows remarkably little fish eaten in inland Britain even near rivers, which may indicate a taboo.

Feasting mattered enormously and was political: the champion's portion, the seating order, who pours. Imported wine amphorae in elite graves show a Mediterranean trade running long before the legions.

Body ◆

Health, Age and Appearance

Life expectancy at birth was low — perhaps the mid-thirties — but dominated by infant mortality; survive childhood and sixty was attainable. Skeletons show heavy dental wear from stone-ground grain, osteoarthritis from labour, healed fractures, and evidence of successful trepanation with bone regrowth around the hole. Somebody knew what they were doing.

Textiles were wool and linen, dyed with woad, madder and weld — Iron Age dress was colourful and patterned, not the undyed homespun of illustration. Lime-washed hair, moustaches, elaborate brooches and torcs on those who could afford them.

Sacred Space ◆

Groves and Water

The word nemeton, sacred grove, survives across the Celtic world from Galatia to Scotland. Lucan describes a grove near Massilia so dreadful that even the priests feared to enter at noon — literary, but the institution is corroborated.

Archaeology shows rectangular ditched enclosures (Gournay-sur-Aronde, Ribemont) with deliberate deposits of weapons, animal bone and human remains. And above all water: swords, cauldrons, torcs and shields thrown into rivers, lakes and bogs — not lost, but given, bent and broken first so they could not be retrieved. Llyn Cerrig Bach on Anglesey held more than a hundred and fifty such objects.

✦

What They Held

Belief, as Far as We Can Reach It

The soul does not die ◆

This is the single most consistently reported druidic doctrine, and it is reported by writers who found it startling. Caesar: they wish to inculcate that souls do not perish, but pass after death from one to another. He adds, drily, that this is excellent for courage in battle. Lucan says the same, and finds it remarkable that these people regard death as the midpoint of a long life.

Whether this is reincarnation in the Indian sense, or passage to another world, or transmigration between bodies, the sources do not agree — and Greek writers kept assimilating it to Pythagoras, which tells you more about Greek writers than about druids.

The Otherworld ◆

From Irish literature: a land alongside this one, reached through a mound, a mist, a lake or a particular night of the year. Time runs differently there. Food and beauty are inexhaustible. It is not a reward for virtue and not a punishment — it is simply next door. Much later, this becomes the faery tradition, and later still, as the Extraterrestrials page argues, something else again.

Many gods, locally ◆

Over four hundred Celtic deity names are attested in inscriptions, and the overwhelming majority occur exactly once — tied to a spring, a hill, a river, a tribe. A handful are widespread: Lugus, Epona, Taranis, Cernunnos. Roman writers mapped them onto their own gods, which has buried the originals under a Latin overlay we cannot always lift.

The mistletoe rite ◆

Pliny's famous passage: on the sixth day of the moon, a white-robed priest cuts mistletoe from an oak with a golden sickle, catching it in a white cloak, and two white bulls are sacrificed. It is one paragraph, written by a Roman naturalist who never saw it, describing something he calls rare — and it has supplied nearly the entire visual vocabulary of druidry ever since. Gold is also too soft to cut with.

✦

Not Romanticised

The Sacrifice Question

Roman sources say the druids practised human sacrifice, including Caesar's wicker man filled with living victims. The order will neither repeat this as fact nor wave it away.

Against: it is exactly the atrocity story an invader needs, Rome itself had banned its own human sacrifice only recently, and the wicker man appears in no other source and no archaeology whatsoever.

For: the bog bodies are difficult to explain otherwise. Lindow Man, found in Cheshire in 1984, had been struck twice on the head, garrotted, had his throat cut and was placed face down in a pool — a threefold death that matches patterns in Irish literature. His last meal included charred bread. Tollund Man, Grauballe Man, Old Croghan Man and dozens of others across northern Europe show comparable deliberate, ritualised killing, often of well-fed people with uncalloused hands.

◆ The honest reading: ritual killing happened in Iron Age north-west Europe. Whether druids performed it, how often, and whether victims were criminals, captives or chosen nobles, we do not know. It was probably rare and probably real.

And the necessary note: no part of this order admires it. A tradition worth studying is not a tradition that must be defended in every particular.

✦

The One Document

The Coligny Calendar

The closest thing we have to a druidic text, and it is a bronze fragment of a timetable.

Found near Lyon in 1897: a bronze plate of around the second century CE, in Gaulish written with Latin letters, giving a five-year luni-solar calendar of sixty-two months plus two intercalary months to keep lunar reckoning in step with the solar year. Months are marked MAT or ANM — commonly read as favourable and unfavourable — and divided into halves by the word ATENOUX, probably "returning night", at the full moon.

What it proves: ◆ sophisticated, systematic astronomical reckoning in Gaulish hands, with a workable solution to the hardest problem in pre-modern timekeeping. Also that the night preceded the day in reckoning, as Caesar independently reports — which is why Celtic festivals still begin at sunset.

What it does not prove: that the eight-festival Wheel of the Year existed. The calendar names SAMONIOS, plausibly related to Samhain, and that is close to the extent of the overlap.

✦

The Ending

How Rome Finished Them

It was deliberate, legislated and swift — and it tells you that Rome understood exactly what the druids were. Rome tolerated almost every religion it conquered. It did not tolerate this one, because the druids were not primarily a religion. They were the pan-tribal institution through which Gaul could coordinate against an occupier.

  • c. 14 CE Augustus bars Roman citizens from druidism.
  • c. 43 CE Claudius abolishes it outright — Suetonius says he suppressed the druidic religion among the Gauls with complete thoroughness.
  • 60 or 61 CE Suetonius Paulinus crosses to Anglesey. Tacitus describes the shore lined with black-robed women waving torches and druids raising their hands in curse; the legionaries hesitate, then advance. The groves are cut down and the island's sanctuaries destroyed. Boudica rises behind them.
  • 2nd–4th c. References dwindle to fortune-tellers and village seers. The learned class is gone as an institution.

Ireland, never invaded, kept its filid and brehons — poets and jurists with recognisably druidic functions — into the Christian period, where they were absorbed rather than destroyed. That absorption is why we have the Irish material at all, and why it is wearing a monk's robe.

✦

The Revival, Honestly Dated

  • 1659 John Aubrey first associates stone circles with druids — a guess, and the origin of the whole modern image.
  • 1723 Stukeley's work on Stonehenge cements the error in the public mind for three centuries.
  • 1717 The traditional founding date of the Ancient Order of Druids in London; the evidence for it is poor.
  • 1792 Iolo Morganwg holds the first Gorsedd on Primrose Hill — and forges a large body of "ancient" Welsh manuscript material to support it.
  • 1792–1826 Iolo's inventions, including much of the ceremonial still used, enter the tradition and are not fully exposed until the twentieth century.
  • 1946 Ross Nichols and Gerald Gardner in conversation; the modern eight-festival Wheel of the Year is assembled from Irish and Germanic names plus the solstices and equinoxes.
  • 1964 The Order of Bards, Ovates and Druids founded.
  • 2010 Druidry is formally recognised as a religion by the Charity Commission for England and Wales.

Which Means What, Exactly

Modern druidry is roughly 250 years old, substantially invented, partly forged, and a genuine and valuable living tradition. Those statements do not conflict, and the order is firm that pretending otherwise damages it.

Every tradition was invented by somebody. Christianity assembled its festival calendar; the kilt and clan tartan date from the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries; most national traditions are younger than the railways. Invention is how traditions begin — the dishonesty is only in claiming an unbroken line that is not there.

What modern druidry does well is real: seasonal attention, ecological ethics as practice rather than opinion, a relationship with specific places and specific trees, ceremony for births and deaths that secular life handles badly, and a posture of reverence that does not require belief in anything unevidenced.

Say "we do this, and we have done it since 1964, and it works" and you have both the practice and your integrity. Say "the ancients did this" and you have surrendered the second for nothing.

✦

A Practice You Can Defend

  • Learn one wood properly. The same half-mile, weekly, for a year. Not a pilgrimage site — the nearest one. You will know more of what the druids knew than any book can give you.
  • Learn twenty trees by bark and bud, not by leaf. Leaf identification is a four-month skill; bark and silhouette work in February.
  • Keep the sunset reckoning. Begin your festival the evening before, as the Coligny calendar and Caesar both indicate. It changes the feel of it entirely.
  • Memorise something long. A poem of two hundred lines, held properly. It is the only part of the original training still available to you, and it is harder and stranger than you expect.
  • Give something to water — and then, unlike the Iron Age, take the litter out with you.
  • Date your sources aloud. When you use Iolo's ceremony, say it is Iolo's. The practice survives the honesty; it is strengthened by it.

Reading

  • ◆ Ronald Hutton, Blood and Mistletoe: The History of the Druids in Britain (2009) — the definitive account, by a historian sympathetic to modern pagans and unsparing about the evidence.
  • ◆ Barry Cunliffe, The Ancient Celts (2nd ed. 2018) — the archaeology, from the leading authority.
  • ◆ Miranda Aldhouse-Green, Caesar's Druids (2010) — careful work on the sources and the sacrifice question.
  • ◆ Caesar, Gallic War, Book VI, 13–18 — the single most important text. Four pages. Read it yourself and notice how little is actually there.
  • ◆ Tacitus, Annals XIV — the destruction of Anglesey, in the words of the conquerors.
  • ◆ Ronald Hutton, The Triumph of the Moon (1999) — the honest history of the modern revival.
  • ◆ The Mabinogion and the Táin — read as literature of genius written by Christians about a remembered pagan world, which is what they are.
