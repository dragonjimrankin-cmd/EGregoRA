## A plain-language route through Part VI

Part VI began with a large archive about the Riemann Hypothesis. It contained fifty-two files, repeated versions, ambitious theorem labels, numerical experiments and later corrections. The task was not to combine every page into one longer claim. It was to work out which statements survived their own revision history.

The Riemann Hypothesis is one of mathematics' most famous open problems. Very roughly, it says that the non-obvious zeros of a function connected to the prime numbers all lie on one central line in the complex plane. That sentence is short, but a proof must control infinitely many zeros, not merely a large sample. It must also avoid assuming the very arrangement it is meant to prove.

This essay describes the identity gap between exact formulas and the missing global implication, follows the correction of several proof routes, and asks whether any surviving result helps TTI's Skeletal Splice. Equations are kept to a minimum.

Likewise, finite checks of Laguerre inequalities, Hankel determinants, Turán gaps or zero approximants demonstrate that an implementation can reproduce expected local behaviour. They do not establish the all-orders or all-heights condition. Precision changes the quality of a finite statement; it does not change its quantifier.

## Why the famous problem is difficult

Prime numbers are introduced through a simple rule, but their gaps do not follow a simple repeating pattern. The zeta function lets mathematicians study their distribution as a whole. Its non-obvious zeros behave rather like frequencies in a complicated signal. The Riemann Hypothesis says that all of those zeros lie on one central line. Computers have checked immense numbers of cases, but no finite computation reaches every zero. A proof needs a reason without an upper limit.

Complex numbers add another difficulty. They have both a real and an imaginary direction, so a sign that is controlled along one path can change when the path turns. The archive's real-axis log-convexity result benefits from positive weights. On the critical line the corresponding factor oscillates. The real-axis theorem remains valid, but crossing from it to the critical line requires a new bridge.

Many proposed criteria translate zero location into positivity of a sequence, matrix, density or quadratic form. Such translations can be profound while leaving the central difficulty untouched. If proving the new positivity is exactly as hard as proving RH, the criterion has reorganised the problem rather than solved it. Reorganisation is still useful, but its status must be stated honestly.

## An evidence ladder

At the bottom are definitions and standard facts. Above those are exact internal calculations. Higher are statements that follow only if RH is assumed, then finite computations, heuristic interpretations and conjectures. At the top are claims later corrected or still carrying an unresolved gap. A correction-aware audit keeps each result on the rung its evidence supports.

Conditional reasoning is legitimate when its condition remains visible. Trouble begins when a formula derived after placing every zero on the critical line is later used to force every zero onto that line. The premise has then entered the conclusion. Finite verification has a related limit: it can test implementations and reveal patterns, but infinity always leaves another case beyond the last computation.

The complete-monotonicity route shows why revisions propagate. An early formula made every term look positive. A later file correctly replaced it with a formula containing competing pieces. The attractive term-by-term proof disappeared. Complete monotonicity may still be true, but it now needs a new proof that controls the full combination for every derivative and input.

The density route failed at a small-parameter inequality. The document claimed that only one early term could be negative, but more terms enter the negative block as the parameter shrinks. Positive samples remain evidence about those samples. They do not certify every point or the limiting region. A repair needs rigorous finite enclosures plus a bound on the infinite tail.

The Pólya-kernel route used the shape of a positive kernel. Its variance sketch did not control the unbounded tail needed for a global theorem. The critical-line converse had a different problem: one negative off-line contribution need not dominate every positive background contribution. A later detectability discussion did not repair that universal domination step.

## What RH can and cannot give the splice

TTI's splice and the RH archive both use sequences, transforms, spectra and positivity. Shared vocabulary creates a possible research conversation, not an implication. The splice still needs a unique action map explaining whether its numbers are fields, sources, probabilities, boundary data or something else. The RH work supplies no selection rule.

Gravity also needs a physical scale. Prime numbers, zeta zeros and splice ratios are dimensionless in ordinary mathematical use. A dimensionless pattern can determine a ratio but cannot create metres, seconds or joules without independent physical input. Electromagnetism similarly needs field normalisation and a charge spectrum. A circular phase can motivate symmetry without fixing the measured coupling.

The halo proposal needs an independent starting radius. Choosing a prime or zero because it improves a galaxy fit would move the target after seeing the data. A genuine prediction requires one universal anchor or one preregistered rule tied to separately measured formation information. It must also explain both motion and lensing with the same gravitational source.

RH contributes method rather than constants. Transform the exact splice sequence before interpreting it. Define the infinite operator before computing finite matrices. Keep an error budget smaller than the measured margin. Register the scale, phase and statistic before examining astronomical data. These rules do not solve the splice, but they make a future claim harder to fit retrospectively.

## A responsible publication route

The first paper should state the narrowest independently checkable result. Exact balance identities can be supporting lemmas without being advertised as evidence of RH. Complete monotonicity, density positivity and kernel log-concavity should remain conjectures until their missing global bounds are supplied. Numerical tables should include code, precision, truncation rules and certified error budgets.

A correction table should list each original statement, later change, downstream consequence and present status. This is not an embarrassment. It shows that the research has learned. Mathematical and cosmological publications should remain separate until a genuine bridge exists. Cross-reference is proper when one programme supplies a method; implication should be claimed only when the assumptions of one actually entail a result in the other.

For a general reader, every paper should begin by saying what was proved, what was assumed and what remains open. Technical sections can carry the details without hiding the scope. If a result depends on RH, say so in the sentence that states it. If it is numerical, name the finite range. If novelty is unverified, do not advertise discovery. Clear scope is part of a proof's public meaning.

## How an average reader can audit a difficult proof

A reader does not need to reproduce every advanced calculation to understand the logical shape of a proof. Begin by writing the main claim in ordinary language. Does it concern every zero, every positive input or every derivative? Universal words matter because a thousand successful examples do not cover an unlimited domain. If the conclusion is universal, locate the step that also has universal reach.

Next, list the assumptions before following the calculations. Some may be standard definitions. Others may be temporary conditions such as assuming RH. A conditional theorem is valuable, but its condition must not vanish from the conclusion. If a later paragraph uses an all-on-line representation while advertising an unconditional result, the proof has crossed its own boundary.

Then identify the exact object being studied. Similar-looking functions can have different derivatives, transforms and signs. In the archive, replacing one function with a simpler positive sum made a proof easy, but the replacement was not exact. Checking the formula before checking the estimate prevents many pages of valid algebra from being applied to the wrong object.

Look for changes of order. Infinite sums, derivatives and integrals cannot always be exchanged freely. A paper should state why the exchange converges in the region being used. If the argument later moves to a boundary or a different direction in the complex plane, the convergence statement may need to be proved again. Analytic continuation can extend a function without preserving the original integral as a directly convergent formula everywhere.

When a smooth model replaces discrete data, ask for the error term. Smooth zero density can explain an average tendency, while individual zeros still create fluctuations. The main term decides a sign only when the remainder is smaller than the margin. If leading terms cancel, a rough approximation becomes less trustworthy precisely where it is being asked to do more work.

For computer evidence, record what was finite. How many zeros were used? What matrix size? Which interval was sampled? How many digits of precision? What upper bound covers omitted terms? A tiny positive answer is not informative when the truncation or rounding uncertainty is larger. Certified intervals and reproducible code turn a persuasive picture into a finite mathematical statement.

Finally, follow corrections downstream. If a premise changes, mark every result that cites it. Some conclusions may have independent proofs and survive. Others return to open status. This dependency check is the heart of the consolidated dossier. It is also a general research habit that TTI can apply to physical claims, where a changed scale, coupling or conservation rule can alter many later predictions.

The reader's aim is not to catch the author out. It is to discover exactly what kind of progress has occurred. A corrected formula, a failed numerical route and a surviving lemma can all be valuable outcomes. Clear classification lets later work begin from the strongest point actually reached rather than from the most confident sentence in the archive.

A useful final check is to imagine the claim being false. Which line of the proof would prevent a counterexample? If the answer is only that no counterexample appeared in a search, the result is numerical evidence. If a positive contribution is meant to overwhelm every negative one, where is the inequality that compares the complete totals? If an asymptotic trend is meant to control a middle interval, where do the regions overlap? These questions translate technical gaps into ordinary logic.

The same approach helps with novelty. A derivation can be correct without being new. Establishing priority requires searching equivalent theorems under different notation, checking standard references and asking specialists who know the surrounding literature. The archive's real-axis result is therefore described as internally reproducible while novelty remains unverified. That wording protects both achievements: the argument can be evaluated on its merits, and historical credit is not assigned before the comparison is complete.

This patient style of reading is especially important for famous open problems. The prestige of RH can make a new criterion feel more decisive than it is, while scepticism can make a valid partial result seem worthless. The evidence ladder avoids both reactions. It permits exact lemmas, conditional theorems, finite computations and conjectures to coexist without pretending they carry the same weight. Progress becomes a map of dependencies rather than a single dramatic verdict.

For TTI, that map is the lasting contribution of Part VI. The project now has a worked example of how to consolidate revisions, preserve valid chains and expose unresolved bridges. Applying the same method to gravity, consciousness and cosmic return will not settle those subjects automatically. It will ensure that future advances are stated at the level they have actually earned.

That standard also makes collaboration easier. A mathematician can work on the missing bound without endorsing the cosmology. A physicist can test the splice action without accepting an RH analogy. An astronomer can examine a preregistered residual without taking a position on metaphysics. By separating dependencies, the programme creates smaller questions that different specialists can answer independently and later reconnect if the evidence warrants it. That is a practical form of progress even before a final theorem appears.
