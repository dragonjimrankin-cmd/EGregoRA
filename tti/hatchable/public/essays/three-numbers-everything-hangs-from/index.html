Three Numbers Everything Hangs From

If you wanted to hang a structure from as few hooks as possible, you would look for the points that do not move. There are three of them, and this essay is about why each one is fixed — and what the framework has not yet paid for.

Hooks that do not move

Suppose you were building a mobile — the hanging sort, with arms and weights, the kind that turns slowly above a cot. You would want a hook. Not because hooks are interesting, but because everything else is going to hang from something, and whatever you hang from had better not be moving. The whole engineering question of a mobile is where to attach it, and the answer is always: attach it to the thing that does not swing.

Mathematics has hooks of exactly this kind, and they are called fixed points. A fixed point is a value that an operation leaves alone. You put it in, you do the operation, and what comes out is the same value you put in. It did not move. That is not a boring property; it is the reason fixed points are the organising skeleton of an entire branch of mathematics, because any structure that is invariant under an operation is built from the points the operation leaves alone.

The framework’s claim in this chapter is that there are three such points, that everything on the site hangs from them, and that each of the three is fixed for a different reason — which is what makes them a set rather than a coincidence. The three are zero, one and minus one. Here is what each one does.

Why zero is the one nobody lands on

Before the three hooks are put to work, one of them needs defending, because a reader who has followed the essay about returning beings will have noticed something odd. Two of the three points are places things go. One is not.

Zero is fixed under scaling precisely because it is absorbing: multiply anything by zero and the anything is gone. That is a peculiar kind of stability, and it is different in character from the stability of one and minus one. One is fixed because multiplication by one changes nothing — it is the point of no change. Minus one is fixed because a half-turn brings you back to it — it is the point of return. Zero is fixed because it eats whatever is sent at it. Three kinds of immobility, and only one of them is a place you could stand.

Which is why the framework insists that nothing lands on zero. Zero is the floor: not a rung, not a destination, not a state a being can occupy. A cell in its resting state is not at zero; it is at the level it is working at, uncommitted. A sent-back being lands on the skeleton, not on the floor. And the reason this matters is that the framework’s opening chain would break if zero were a place — the first link says zero is the floor no shrinking quantity reaches, and a floor you could reach would be a wall at the bottom of the world rather than a limit. So the arithmetic and the story agree, and the agreement is worth noticing: the point that absorbs everything is the one point nothing arrives at, because arrival would make it a destination.

What the three points do for the chain

To see why the framework cares, it helps to look at what happens without them.

The chain that the whole site is built on runs: zero and one are two infinities; they are unequal; a straight path between them never closes; so the path must curve; the constant of curving is π; the closing shape is a torus; and a change of side without crossing is a half-twist. Every one of those steps assumes something about the endpoints. If zero were not fixed under scale, it would not be a floor — you could scale your way out of it, or into it. If one were not fixed under multiplication, it would not be a threshold — multiplying by one would change something, which is absurd on its face. And if minus one were not fixed under the half-turn, the return would not be a return: the operation that turns a thing around would land you somewhere slightly different every time, which is a spiral, not a closure.

So the three points are not decoration on the chain. They are what makes each link hold. The framework puts it in the sharpest form it can: everything the site claims is an elaboration of an integration that runs between two ends, and an integration needs ends that do not move. There are exactly three points in the arithmetic that do not move in the required ways, and the framework takes the matching as the strongest structural evidence it has. ◆ The mathematics of fixed points is standard and old; it is the framework’s choice to read the universe through it.

The place where this becomes vivid, and the place where a reader should start checking, is the skeleton: the claim that the three points recur at scale. Place the counting numbers on the framework’s log spiral and most of them land wherever they land. But some land on the fixed points’ images, and the framework’s reading is that those recurrences are the joints at which structure is spliced — thirty-three, three hundred and thirty-three, six hundred and sixty-six, nine hundred and ninety-nine. The full name of the splice comes from this: the Rankin Skeletal Splice, a fork made at the joints of the skeleton rather than anywhere along the bone.

Six hundred and sixty-six, and the thirty-sixth triangle

The middle of those three numbers has an arithmetic curiosity attached, and the framework reports it because it is exact, not because it is eerie.

Triangular numbers are what you get if you stack counters in rows: one, then three, then six, then ten, each one adding a row to the one before. The formula is the familiar one, n times n plus one divided by two. Run it to n = 36 and the value is 666. So six hundred and sixty-six is the thirty-sixth triangular number — a fact about stacked counters, checkable in a moment on paper. ◆

Now the second half, which is where the framework’s reading enters. Thirty-six is also a physical count: six flavours of quark, times three colours, times two matter and antimatter states, equals thirty-six. So the number of quark states in the standard model is the index at which the triangular series produces the framework’s density point. ◆ The framework reports this as a coincidence it does not explain — and the word “coincidence” is being used precisely. Six and three and two are chosen by nature, not by the arithmetic, and nothing in the framework forces the quark count to be what it is. The framework’s own view is that the matching is either a coincidence or a datum, and that a reader is entitled to treat it as the former until something more than a matching appears.

There is a discipline being demonstrated here that is worth more than the coincidence. The framework could have written this paragraph to sound like a discovery. It is written instead with the two halves labelled: triangular numbers are arithmetic; the quark count is physics; the correspondence is an observation. That is the pattern for the whole chapter — take something exact, take something measured, and say plainly which is which and what the gap between them costs. ◆

Fixed points everywhere else, and why that is the framework's best argument

Here is the strongest thing that can be said for this chapter, and it does not depend on any of the framework’s unusual claims.

The habit of finding the thing that does not move and hanging a description from it is not a private taste. It is how physics has worked for four centuries, and the examples are not marginal.

In dynamics, the behaviour of a system is organised by its fixed points: the places where nothing changes. A pendulum has two — hanging straight down, and balanced exactly upright — and the whole qualitative behaviour of the pendulum, from gentle swinging to the infinite-period separatrix to whirling, is described by the relationship between those two points and the curves that flow between them. The fixed points are not among the interesting behaviours; they are the coordinates in which the interesting behaviours are described. ◆

In symmetry, the same move appears in its most powerful form. Emmy Noether showed in 1918 that every continuous symmetry of a physical system corresponds to a quantity that does not change: symmetry under shifts in time gives conservation of energy, symmetry under shifts in space gives conservation of momentum, symmetry under rotation gives conservation of angular momentum. ◆ That is a theorem of enormous reach, and its content is exactly the habits of this chapter: find the thing that does not move, and you have found what the structure is made of.

In mathematics, fixed points are the standard tool for existence: a great many existence proofs work by showing that an operation must have a point it leaves alone, and that point is the solution. And in statistics, the notion of equilibrium — the distribution a system settles towards — is a fixed point of the process that drives it.

So the framework’s instinct is not eccentric. It is the instinct of the field, applied to the framework’s own subject matter. What is eccentric is the claim that the three specific points are the ones the universe hangs from, and the eccentricity is where the work remains to be done — which is precisely the difference between an argument that a method is sound and an argument that a particular application of it is true. The chapter knows which it has. It has the first completely, and the second not yet.

What the chapter owes, stated plainly

Two debts belong to this chapter and neither is small, so both are named in the chapter’s own voice rather than extracted by an unfriendly reader.

The first is the base-ten problem, and it is the more serious of the two. The framework reads density as a place value: accumulation past the threshold of one carries, the way a digit carries when you count past nine. That reading is exact and beautiful in base ten, and it is only exact in base ten. In base eight, the carry happens at eight; in base sixteen, at sixteen. If the reading is to be a claim about the world rather than a claim about our notation, it needs a base-independent form, and no such form has been written. The framework’s best defence is that the decimal system is the one in which counting is most naturally taught and the one in which place value is most visible, and that defence explains the appeal of the reading rather than the truth of it. Until a base-free version exists, the density-as-place-value reading sits on the framework’s chosen pile, and it is listed among the things owed on the summary.

The second debt is the skeleton’s physical status. The three fixed points are arithmetic. The recurrences — thirty-three, three hundred and thirty-three, six hundred and sixty-six, nine hundred and ninety-nine — are arithmetic too, given the spiral and the mapping. But the claim that the universe splices at those joints is a claim about the world, and there is no measurement of it. Nobody has measured a splice. There is no experiment that could distinguish a universe that splices at the skeleton from one that splices wherever it likes, because the framework has not specified what a splice would look like from outside. That is the honest position, and it is the difference between a well-constructed mathematical picture and a physical theory.

Why this chapter is the floor of the building

Every structure has a storey that everything else rests on, and in this framework it is this one.

The arithmetic essay hangs on the recursion, and the recursion hangs on the threshold; the threshold is one of the three points. The half-twist hangs on the idea that a return can happen without a crossing, and the return is the half-turn, which is the third point. The sent-back beings hang on there being places for them to land, and those places are the skeleton, which is the three points recurring. The dark-sector chart hangs on the exchange between two phases, and the phases are read through zero and one. If the three points were not fixed — if the arithmetic did not work out as it does — almost nothing else in this framework would have anything to hang from, and the site would be a set of suggestive essays rather than a structure.

That is worth saying because it tells the reader where a single blow would be fatal. Attacking the dark sector might cost a chapter. Attacking the dephasing account might cost an amendment. Attacking the fixed points costs the building, because every floor above them is standing on them. And here is the countervailing fact, which is the framework’s genuine strength and the reason it survives being attacked at all: the fixed points are not the framework’s invention. Zero really is fixed under scaling. One really is fixed under multiplication. Minus one really is where a half-turn returns you. A mathematician who has never heard of this site would agree with all three sentences, and would be puzzled to hear that anything was at stake in them.

So the chapter’s honest summary of itself is this. The hooks are real: three points, fixed under three operations, with a mathematics that nobody disputes. The hanging is the framework’s own: that these are the hooks the universe uses, that they recur as a skeleton, and that density carries like a place value in the one base we happen to count in. The hooks are the reason the framework is worth taking seriously. The hanging is the reason it still has to be argued for — and, being the floor, it is also the reason there is somewhere to argue from.
