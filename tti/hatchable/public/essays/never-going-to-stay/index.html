The Universe Was Never Going to Stay This Way

This essay is where honesty requires a correction, and it is an important one. The famous 68 : 27 : 5 is a photograph, not a law — and the framework once wanted more from it than that.

Three numbers you have seen a hundred times

If you read popular cosmology for more than a fortnight you will meet a pie chart. It divides the universe into three parts: about sixty-eight per cent dark energy, about twenty-seven per cent dark matter, about five per cent ordinary matter — everything you have ever touched, seen, or been. Those three numbers are the standard summary of what the universe is made of, and they are printed on posters, in textbooks, on the covers of magazines, and in this essay’s next figure. ◆

They are also, and this is the whole of this essay, today’s numbers. They describe the universe at the present moment, at one point in thirteen and a half billion years, and the point is not special except that it is where we are standing. Run the clock backwards and the proportions change. Run it forwards and they change again, and in the end the chart becomes almost all one thing. Nothing in the physics suggests the numbers are stable; quite the opposite, the physics suggests they must move. To read 68 : 27 : 5 as a permanent property of the universe is like reading a photograph of a person aged forty and concluding that people are forty.

The framework’s dark-sector chapter argues that two of these three are one thing in two phases. If that is right, the pie has two ingredients rather than three, and the chart becomes a statement about the balance between a field and everything else. That claim is separate from this essay’s subject, which is about time. Whether the dark sector is one field or two substances, the proportions change, and the reason they change is not subtle — it is arithmetic about how different kinds of stuff dilute as space expands.

How anyone knows this at all

It is worth pausing on how three numbers about the entire universe can be known to within a few per cent, because the answer is one of the great achievements of the last thirty years and it is not obvious. Nobody has put the universe on a scale. Instead, three independent kinds of measurement converge, and their agreement is the reason the numbers are trusted.

The first is the cosmic microwave background: the light left over from the moment the universe first became transparent, about 380,000 years in. That light is not uniform. It is mottled — warmer here, cooler there, by parts in a hundred thousand — and the pattern of the mottling encodes the composition of the universe at that moment. How far the blobs extend, how their sizes are distributed, how strongly they are polarised: each of those is a lever on how much matter, how much radiation and how much vacuum energy there is. The cosmic microwave background alone gives a table of proportions, and in 2003 the WMAP satellite published the first version of that table that everyone believed. ◆

The second is the expansion history. In 1998 two competing teams measured the brightness of distant exploding stars and found, to everyone’s surprise, that the expansion of the universe is speeding up rather than slowing down. That result is the reason dark energy exists as a concept at all, and it won a Nobel Prize in 2011. ◆ The third is the pattern of galaxy clustering: sound waves from the early universe left a preferred distance in the distribution of galaxies, a standard ruler, which shows up in surveys of millions of galaxies and again pins the proportions.

Three methods, three sets of assumptions, one table. When methods that share almost no machinery agree to within a few per cent, that is evidence. It does not make the numbers permanent, and the whole of this essay is about why they are not permanent — but it does make them real, and worth arguing about.

Why the numbers must move

There is a simple picture that makes the whole thing obvious, and it needs no equations.

Imagine three substances in a stretching space: matter, radiation, and a constant. Matter is stuff that dilutes as the box stretches, because the same number of particles spread through a bigger volume — so its density falls as the inverse cube of the size. Radiation dilutes faster: not only do the photons spread out, they also lose energy as the space stretches them — so radiation falls as the inverse fourth power. And dark energy, whatever it is, behaves as though it were a property of space itself rather than stuff in it: stretch the box and the density stays the same, which means the total amount grows with the volume. ◆

Put those three behaviours on the same chart and they cannot stay in balance. Early on, when the universe was small and hot, radiation dominated — it dilutes fastest, so it is highest when everything is densest. Then radiation falls below matter, and matter dominates for a long stretch of the universe’s middle age, because matter falls more slowly than radiation and the constant is negligible while the universe is small. Then, eventually, the constant overtakes matter: because matter keeps diluting and the constant does not, the crossing is inevitable and happens once. We are living a little after that crossing. In cosmological terms, quite close to it, which is why the balance looks so even today and why the chart is interesting rather than lopsided.

So the composition of the universe is not a fixed property but a stage. Go back far enough and the chart reads: radiation, overwhelming; matter, a minority; dark energy, nothing. Go forward far enough and the chart reads: dark energy, nearly everything; matter, a fading trace; radiation, gone. Today’s three numbers sit at the interesting moment, which is one reason cosmologists have spent so long arguing about whether that is a coincidence or whether something selects it. That argument is open, and it is genuinely open — nobody has a satisfying answer, and the framework does not either.

What the framework tried to do, and why it had to stop

Now the correction, in the framework’s own voice, because it is one of the two changes that cost the most to make.

Earlier versions of this work leaned hard on the three numbers. There is a temptation, when you have a framework that claims to unify opposites and a chart that shows a three-way split, to read the chart as a solution rather than a measurement. More than that: to derive it. If the universe is an integration of two infinities, and the dark sector is one field with a value, then perhaps the value at the present epoch is fixed by the structure — perhaps 68 : 27 : 5 is not an accident of when we happen to be looking but a number the geometry requires. Earlier drafts went some distance down that road, treating the proportions as something the framework could explain and aiming to produce them from the mathematics.

That project has been withdrawn, and the reason is plain. The proportions are not fixed by anything in the structure, because they depend on when you look. There is no single number to derive. A framework can derive a constant; it cannot derive a photograph. Any derivation would have to explain not only the value but the epoch, and explaining the epoch is answering the coincidence problem, which is the problem rather than the answer. The old claim was not merely unproved; it was the wrong shape of claim, and recognising that is the correction this chapter records. ◆

What survives the correction

A correction that destroys everything is a failure; a correction that destroys a claim and leaves a structure standing is what science looks like doing its job. So what survives here?

The phase reading survives, in modified form. The dark sector can still be one field in two phases, because that claim was never about the current proportions — it was about behaviour: how the field acts in a collision (the Bullet Cluster), how it falls off across a galaxy (the flat rotation curves). Those tests do not care what year it is. What does not survive is any suggestion that the framework predicts how much of each phase there is.

The arithmetic of accumulation survives, because it never depended on the chart. The splice is exact, and it does not mention dark energy, dark matter, or the present epoch. That is the advantage of having one claim that is arithmetic and another that is cosmology: when the cosmology moves, the arithmetic stays where it is.

The arrow of time discussion survives in a form its author had to revise twice, which this chapter touches on because the topic overlaps. The original argument read the cosmic asymmetry as a direction: more room in one direction, therefore a lean, therefore an arrow. That was withdrawn — the asymmetry suggests an oscillation rather than a direction — and the work of carrying irreversibility was moved to a different mechanism entirely, one that has nothing to do with the pie chart. The reader who wants that story in full should read the last essay in this series, which is where the framework is most nearly honest about how much of its original argument it has had to give up.

The present-moment reading also survives in a smaller way, and it is worth naming because it is genuinely attractive. If the universe is an integration that never completes, then the present moment is not a moving spotlight travelling along a finished timeline. It is the front edge where the accumulating meets the arriving, which is the framework’s reading of the seam. 68 : 27 : 5 is then a description of the front edge at one instant — the shape of the wave at one point in its travel. Nobody should be surprised that a shape measured mid-journey is not constant. The surprise was that the framework ever expected it to be.

The coincidence, stated fairly

If the three eras are stages and we are living near a handover between two of them, a question follows immediately: is that an extraordinary coincidence, or is there some reason to expect it?

Stated in its strongest form, the puzzle is this. Matter dilutes and dark energy does not, so the universe spends most of its history with one of them dominant, and crosses from one to the other only once. The crossing is brief — cosmologically brief, perhaps a couple of billion years of interesting balance in a history that will run for trillions. We live in it. If you calculate the fraction of the universe’s timeline in which the chart looks like a three-part pie, you get a small number. That is the coincidence problem, and it is a real puzzle about real measurements.

There are three standard responses, and they are worth having on the table because the framework’s own position is the third.

The first is that it is not a coincidence at all: some unknown mechanism couples the two densities and keeps them comparable, and finding that mechanism is the job of whoever is cleverest in the next fifty years. This is a respectable programme with a lot of failed attempts behind it. The second is anthropic — observers require galaxies, galaxies require matter to dominate long enough to collapse, and so any observer that exists at all finds itself in the window where structure is possible. That argument is not stupid and it is not beloved, because it explains the observation without predicting anything.

The third is the framework’s, and it is a refusal rather than an answer: the epoch is not derivable from the structure, so the framework does not attempt to derive it. That position is honest and it is also unsatisfying — a refusal is not a solution — and the framework marks it as an open item rather than as content. What makes the refusal defensible is that the other two responses are, so far, theories with no verdict. When a field has two live explanations and neither has produced a measurable consequence, declining to add a third is not laziness. It is keeping the record clean, which this framework, having just withdrawn a claim, has some reason to care about.

The far future, and the reason it matters to a reader in 2026

One more consequence of the arithmetic deserves its figure, because it is the most concrete thing in this essay and because it is strange enough to be worth the room.

Run the clock forward and dark energy wins, and it does not win narrowly. Because matter dilutes and dark energy does not, the gap widens without limit, and the universe approaches a state in which essentially all of the energy density is dark energy — with matter, radiation and structure as vanishing traces. In that far future, the galaxies that remain gravitationally bound to us will be the Local Group; everything beyond it will have receded past reach. Distant galaxies will not merely be faint. They will be unobservable in principle, because the space between us and them expands faster than light can cross it. An astronomer in that era, doing everything right, would find a universe containing one modest group of galaxies and no evidence at all of a larger structure.

That far-future picture matters to this framework for one reason and to cosmology for another. To cosmology, it is a reminder that the universe is not built for observers: the epoch in which a three-colour pie chart is available is a narrow window, and we happen to be in it. To this framework, it is a reminder of its own thesis, read correctly. The universe is not settling down; the integration is not converging on a finished state; the proportions keep moving because the terms of the sum keep changing. A framework that reads existence as an unfinished integral should be unsurprised when the bookkeeping will not sit still.

And the honesty, stated as this chapter states it: this essay’s contribution to the framework is a subtraction. It removes a claim. It does not replace it with a better claim, and it does not claim credit for the removal. What it does is leave the rest of the structure with one fewer thing to defend — which, when you are building something that expects to be attacked, is progress of the only kind that lasts.
