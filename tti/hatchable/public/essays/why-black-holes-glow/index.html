Why Black Holes Glow

Hawking’s 1974 result, told carefully and without dressing it up — and then the question the chapter actually asks: why does the shape of that story look so familiar?

In this essay

  1. The most famous sentence in modern physics, unpacked

  4. The temperature ladder, and why size is everything
  5. The largest entropy in the universe, and a laboratory echo

The most famous sentence in modern physics, unpacked

In 1974 Stephen Hawking published a calculation that said something nobody expected and which he himself resisted: black holes are not entirely black. ◆ They glow. Not brightly, and not for any useful purpose, but they glow — and the glow has a temperature, and the temperature depends on the hole.

Two years before, in 1972, Jacob Bekenstein had already argued that a black hole must have entropy. It sounds harmless and was not. Entropy is a count of how many arrangements of the parts would look the same from the outside, and a black hole has — according to classical general relativity — no parts at all, only a horizon and whatever is inside it, and no way for the outside to know which is which. If there is no way to tell two black holes apart except by mass, spin and charge, then the number of arrangements that look identical is one, and one arrangement means zero entropy. Bekenstein said that could not be right, because dropping a cup of tea into a black hole would reduce the entropy of the universe, and the second law of thermodynamics does not have exceptions. Hawking set out to prove Bekenstein wrong. He proved him right, and in the process found that the hole radiates. ◆

Here is the mechanism, in the form it is usually told, and this essay will come back to how much of it to trust.

The temperature, for the curious, is tiny for any hole of astronomical size: a black hole the mass of the sun has a temperature of about six hundredths of a microkelvin, far colder than the cosmic microwave background, so real black holes absorb far more than they emit. Smaller holes are hotter — the temperature goes as one over the mass — and a hole of a few billion tonnes would be hot enough to glow in gamma rays. This is Hawking’s result and nobody seriously disputes it. Where the argument starts is in exactly one place, and it is a place worth naming precisely.

What the popular story gets wrong

The trouble with the pair-of-particles story is that it takes a metaphor and hands it to you as a mechanism. The real calculation is about fields on a curved background, and what it shows is that the vacuum — the definition of “nothing there” — is different for an observer who is accelerating and an observer who is not. An accelerating observer sees a warm bath of particles where a still observer sees emptiness. Since holding still just outside a horizon requires accelerating hard, the two observers disagree about what the vacuum contains, and the disagreement is what Hawking computed. No pair is born in the mathematics. Nothing is split. What is real is a temperature, an entropy, and an energy flow.

This matters for the chapter, and it matters for how you read the rest of the site. The site’s habit is to grade its claims, and the honest grading here is as follows. Hawking radiation: established physics, with the real derivation being far stranger than the story. ◆ The pair-creation picture: a good teaching device that has become a folk explanation, and which the framework uses for its shapes rather than its physics. The general lesson — that a boundary can be the place where the real business happens, and that what happens at a boundary can be described as a relationship between two regions that neither region owns alone — is the lesson the framework takes away, and it takes it from the shape of the story.

There is also a genuine unsolved problem attached, and it is the biggest one in theoretical physics. If a black hole evaporates completely, what happened to the information that fell in? Quantum mechanics says information is never destroyed, ever — that is not a preference, it is the substance of the theory. General relativity says the hole disappears and takes its contents with it. Both cannot be right, and the contradiction is called the information paradox. A great deal of very clever work has gone into it since 1974, most of it inconclusive, and the most popular recent answers involve the information being encoded on the horizon — which is to say, on the boundary. ◆

The framework does not claim to solve the information paradox, and it would be ridiculous if it did. What it does is notice that the shape of the leading candidates is the shape of its own central image. When physics needed somewhere to put the information, it put it on the edge.

The shape the framework notices

Now the chapter’s actual subject. Strip out the physics and keep the geometry of Hawking’s picture — the folk picture, the one with the pair — and what you have is this:

A single thing appears at a boundary. The boundary separates it into two, and the two do not recombine. One goes one way, one goes the other, and the total is conserved.

That is the forking being, and the framework has been drawing it since chapter nine. The being that reaches the threshold splits: half climbs to the next level, half is sent back. The conservation is built in — nothing is created and nothing is lost; what looks like a gain of one is a division of two. The framework’s splice is the same shape: at each threshold the accumulated structure forks, and the count of living things follows a recursion rather than a slope.

The claim here is not that Hawking radiation is the forking being under another name. It is not. The claim is that the same shape appears in three places that were arrived at by different routes — in the arithmetic of the tree, in the framework’s reading of the ledger, and in the physics of a horizon — and that a shape recurring is a datum. Not a proof. A datum. When the same geometry turns up in a proof about Fibonacci numbers, in a chapter about accumulated polarity, and in a 1974 calculation about curved spacetime, the reasonable response is neither “this proves the framework” nor “this is coincidence”. It is: note it, write it down, and see whether a fourth appearance turns up somewhere nobody was looking.

That is exactly the posture the chapter takes, and there is a name for the mistake it is avoiding. The mistake is called interpretive drift in this framework’s vocabulary: the slide from “these two things share a shape” to “these two things are the same”. Sharing a shape is cheap, because shapes are common. If a horizon radiates in one direction and the tree forks in another, and both are shaped like a boundary producing two paths, you have learned something about suggestive geometry and nothing about physics.

The temperature ladder, and why size is everything

One more piece of the physics is worth having, because it makes the abstraction tangible and it also takes the framework past a place where it could have embarrassed itself.

Hawking temperature runs as one over mass. That single fact, applied to real objects, produces numbers so extreme that they read as comedy.

The framework uses this ladder in a specific, careful way. The temptation with a temperature that scales inversely with size is to say something grand about the small being hot and the large being cold: about the infinitesimal being full of activity, about accumulation cooling things down, about a universe that gets quieter as it grows. Some of that is true of real objects. Water in a cup cools faster than water in a bath; small animals lose heat faster than big ones; the surface-to-volume ratio is one of the most reliably-appearing quantities in nature, and a black hole’s temperature is a surface effect — it comes from the horizon, which is area, and area is two-thirds of a volume. So the ladder is not mystical. It is geometry: the horizon is a surface, the mass is a lump, and the ratio between surface and lump scales the way it always scales.

That last sentence is the sort of thing this framework does at its best, and it is worth pausing on. It takes a spectacular result, and rather than gasping at it, it asks whether the spectacle is an instance of something ordinary — surface against volume — and answers that it is. The framework then claims no credit for the physics, and reserves its own claim for the shape. That is the right division of labour, and it is why an essay about black holes belongs in a site about two infinities rather than in a site about astronomy.

The largest entropy in the universe, and a laboratory echo

Two more pieces of physics belong here, because they make the chapter honest in both directions: the first shows how much is at stake at a horizon, and the second shows that the strangest prediction in this territory has been seen in a laboratory.

Bekenstein’s result, sharpened by Hawking, is that a black hole’s entropy is a quarter of its horizon area, measured in Planck units — the natural units in which the constants of nature are all one. Four is not a mystical number here; it is the coefficient the calculation produces, and it falls out of the geometry of the horizon. What the formula means in practice is worth stating plainly. For a black hole of a few solar masses, the entropy is around 1077 units, in the same counting units that give a cup of water about 1025. A single black hole therefore holds more entropy than all the ordinary matter in the visible universe, by something like fifty orders of magnitude, and it holds it in a space whose contents are hidden from every observer who will ever exist. ◆

That scale is what makes the information paradox bite rather than merely puzzle. When something falls into a black hole, the entropy of the universe does not drop — it is absorbed, and the hole grows by an amount that pays for it exactly. The bookkeeping balances, which is why Bekenstein’s proposal survived. But if the hole later evaporates to nothing, and the radiation that carries the energy away is featureless thermal noise, then the entropy comes back as randomness and the information is gone. The second law is fine; the reversibility of quantum mechanics is not. Something has to give, and decades of work have not settled which something.

Now the laboratory part, which the framework cites with approval because it is the sort of thing the framework’s method is supposed to prefer. In 2016 Jeff Steinhausen and his colleagues built an analogue: a flowing condensate of cold atoms with a region where the flow went faster than the speed of sound inside it. Sound cannot escape once the current carries it past a certain point, which makes that boundary a horizon for sound rather than for light. If Hawking’s calculation is about horizons rather than specifically about gravity, the sound horizon should behave the same way — and the measurement found a thermal spectrum of phonons at the temperature the calculation predicts. It is not a test of gravity, and nobody claims it is. It is a test of the method, and the method passed. ◆

The framework’s lesson from that experiment is not support and is not meant to be. It is this: when a shape claims to be general — a boundary that splits a pair, a horizon that has a temperature — the way to find out is to build a smaller one and see whether the shape holds. That is the standard the framework’s own predictions are held to, and the chapter states that it has not met it.

What this chapter does not claim, and what would break it

Three things the chapter does not claim, in plain sentences, because a reader should be able to hold the limits of a claim in one hand.

It does not claim Hawking radiation is caused by the forking being, or that the forking being is a mechanism of any kind. The framework has no mechanism for Hawking radiation and does not offer one; the mechanism is quantum field theory on curved spacetime, and it is complete without help.

It does not claim the information paradox is resolved by the shape. The shape is suggestive of where the resolution lies — on the boundary, in the encoding, which is where the serious physics has been heading — but suggesting where somebody else’s answer will be found is not the same as giving it, and the framework does not conflate the two.

It does not claim that the recurrence of the shape in three places is evidence. Two shapes matching is not evidence; a thousand shapes matching is also not evidence, because shapes are cheap and eyes are pattern-hungry. What would be evidence is a specific, quantitative coincidence: if some quantity in horizon physics had the value 1.1708, or the population structure Fn+2 − 1, the chapter would have something to point at. It does not, and the framework says so.

What would break the chapter? Very little, and that is the difficulty with it. Because the chapter claims so little — that a shape recurs, that a datum is worth noting — almost nothing can falsify it, and a claim that nothing can falsify is not doing the work of a scientific claim. The framework concedes this in the stress tests, where the chapter is marked as its weakest kind of content: not wrong, but unfalsifiable in its present form. The honest way to read it is as motivation for a search that might later produce something that can be wrong. If nothing does, the chapter quietly becomes literature, and the framework has said so in advance rather than having it extracted by an unfriendly reader.
