The One Edge

Take a strip of paper, give it one half-twist, join the ends: one face, one edge. This essay is about recognising that moment wherever it turns up — and about the discipline of not seeing it everywhere.

A strip of paper, and one small violence

This essay can be done at a table with a strip of paper, and it is better if it is. Cut a strip about three centimetres wide and thirty long. Now do the one thing that is not allowed in ordinary life: give one end a single half-turn — a hundred and eighty degrees, not three hundred and sixty — and tape the ends together. You now have a Möbius strip, the most famous object in topology, and you can make one for the price of a sheet of paper and a piece of tape.

Now run a finger along the edge. Do not lift the finger. Follow the edge all the way round. You arrive back where you started, having traced what you thought were two edges, and you never once had to cross over to the other one. Then run the finger along the surface, in the middle, again without lifting. You cover what looked like two faces in one journey.

That is the whole miracle and it is entirely mechanical. The strip has one side and one edge. It is not a trick of the eye, not a paradox, not a philosophical puzzle: it is a theorem about a real object that you can hold, and it was described independently in 1858 by August Möbius and Johann Listing, two mathematicians who were not looking for mystery but sorting out how to say precisely what a surface is. ◆

The framework takes this object seriously for a reason that has nothing to do with tricks. The half-twist is the cheapest possible way to make a thing that seemed to have two sides turn out to have one — and the framework’s claim is that this occurs in the physical world, at scale, in places where physicists are already used to something odd happening. That claim is contentious. The paper strip is not.

What the word seam means here

The framework uses seam in a precise way, and it is worth stating before the examples, because the word is doing technical work rather than poetic work.

A seam is a place where the inside of a thing and its boundary carry the same information. Not similar information, and not a place where two things meet — a boundary of that ordinary kind is a wall, and walls have two sides, one each. A seam is where that distinction collapses: the description of the outer edge and the description of everything inside contain exactly the same content, so that knowing one is knowing the other, and there is no second side to cross to.

The mathematical form of that idea is Stokes’ theorem, which was not invented for this purpose and needs no help from the framework. Stokes’ theorem says, in one breath, that if you add up a certain kind of quantity over a boundary, you get the same answer as adding up a related quantity over everything the boundary encloses. The boundary is not a summary of the interior and it is not a container for it: it is the interior, seen one dimension down. The Möbius strip provides the visual case; Stokes provides the theorem. ◆

Two consequences follow, and they are the reason the framework bothers with the distinction.

The first is that a seam cannot be crossed, because there is nothing on the other side of it to cross to. If inside and outside are the same information described twice, then the phrase “over there” has no referent. That sounds like mysticism and is not: it is what the one-sidedness of the strip means, made abstract. The second is that a seam cannot leak, in the sense that nothing is lost at it. Whatever is at the edge is already in the body. That is the precise sense in which the framework reads a horizon as a seam rather than as a wall, and it is one of the readings that gets attacked hardest in the stress tests.

The idea has a short, sharp history, and the dates are worth having in view because they show how quickly a mathematical curiosity became a piece of physics. In 1858 the strip is described as a curiosity of geometry. In 1882 the Klein bottle follows, showing that a surface need not have an edge at all. In 1972 Bekenstein proposes that a black hole’s entropy is proportional to the area of its horizon, which the framework reads as a claim about seams arriving in physics; in 1993 Susskind takes the step that turns it into the holographic principle. ◆ One hundred and thirty-five years, from a paper strip to a principle about the information content of the universe.

The attack is fair, and it is answered there rather than here.

The four seams the framework claims

Having defined the word, the chapter puts four candidates on the table. They are graded differently, and a reader should treat them differently, which is the entire point of keeping them in one list rather than dressing them uniformly.

The first entry deserves a paragraph of expansion, because it is the one that keeps company with serious physics. In the 1970s Jacob Bekenstein noticed, and Stephen Hawking confirmed with a calculation, that the entropy of a black hole is proportional to the area of its horizon, not its volume. That is bizarre on its face: entropy usually counts ways of arranging the contents of a region, and contents fill volumes, so entropy should go as volume. Instead the count goes as area — which means the information about everything that fell in can be written on the surface that swallowed it, and there is no more information than the surface can hold. That result, the holographic principle, is one of the few genuinely revolutionary things to have come out of late-twentieth-century theoretical physics, and it is exactly the shape of a seam: the boundary carrying the interior’s content with nothing left over. ◆

Where the chapter gets ambitious, and how it can fail

The chapter does not stop at cataloguing. It makes a bet, and the bet is the framework’s one falsifiable prediction that is not in the dark sector: handedness.

Möbius objects have a handedness in the same way that a screw does. A strip with a right-handed half-twist cannot be deformed into a strip with a left-handed one without cutting it, exactly as a right-handed screw cannot be turned into a left-handed one without inventing a mirror. This is not a metaphor; it is the mathematics of the orthogonal group, and it is the reason a glove does not become its mirror image by being rotated. If the half-twist is a real feature of physical structure, then somewhere there ought to be an asymmetry between the two handednesses, and the framework claims to know where to look: the spin directions of galaxies.

Here is the honest state of that claim, which is unusual and worth reading carefully because the framework does not usually talk like this. Handedness in the sky is not a novel observation: galaxies that spin one way and galaxies that spin the other are both common and have been catalogued for decades. What would be extraordinary is a systematic excess — more of one than the other, across the visible universe, with a preferred axis. Claims of exactly that kind have been made and have mostly not survived scrutiny: the excesses are usually traced to how the catalogue was assembled, or to the motion of our own galaxy relative to the sample, or to the fact that a spiral seen from the far side looks like a mirror of one seen from near. The chapter therefore commits itself to failure conditions in advance, which is the right move and also the bold one:

  1. The excess must survive correction for Doppler and selection effects — the bias that our own motion and our own sampling impose on the counts.
  2. The excess must reach five sigma, the conventional threshold at which particle physicists agree to call something a discovery.
  3. The preferred axis must not line up with our own galactic poles — because if it does, the most likely explanation is that we are looking at ourselves, through the disc of our own galaxy, and not at the universe.

Fail any of the three, and the chapter is finished as a physical claim. That sentence is the framework’s own, and it is unusual in the genre: most speculative systems write their predictions so that any outcome is a success. This one has written a sentence that it cannot survive.

The next turn: no edge at all

There is a relative of the Möbius strip that the framework keeps in view, and it is worth a section because it shows where the logic goes if you keep turning the screw.

The Möbius strip has one side and one edge — but it still has an edge. Turn the screw one more time and you arrive at the Klein bottle: a surface with one side and no edge at all. It cannot be built in three-dimensional space without passing through itself, which is why the standard picture of it looks like a bottle whose neck dives back through its own belly; in four dimensions the same surface sits there quite happily without self-intersection. The Klein bottle is what you get when a surface closes on itself in the other way — one dimension up, one dimension round.

Why does the framework care? Because of a difference in behaviour rather than appearance. A torus — the plain doughnut, no twist — is symmetric in the way a ring is: you can slide it round, flip it left to right, and the geometry does not notice. Nothing about a torus distinguishes a direction of travel from its reverse. The Klein bottle does not have that indifference. Its construction involves a flip, and a flip cannot be undone by any smooth motion of the surface itself: a path taken in one direction and the same path taken in reverse are genuinely different, and the surface knows. In the language physicists use, the Klein bottle can carry an asymmetry that the torus cannot — the same asymmetry that distinguishes matter from antimatter and a process from its mirror. ◆

The framework’s reading is that this is where the two candidates have different work to do. The torus is what closure requires, and the chapter on the torus makes that case on the strongest ground it has: a closed energy surface in mechanics is a torus, and that has been known since Liouville. The Klein bottle is what a twist with no boundary requires, and the framework keeps it as the further horizon — the shape the structure would have if the half-twist were completed rather than left as a seam. The chapter does not claim the universe is a Klein bottle. It claims the difference between the two objects is exactly the difference the framework has been circling: which of return and reversal is the more basic. Read one way you get a doughnut; read the other, a bottle that is inside itself, with no edge to find and no outside to be outside of.

The discipline of not seeing it everywhere

The real risk with an idea like the seam is that it is too good at explaining things. A pattern that can be seen in a horizon, a cell membrane and a moment of experience can also be seen, with a bit of effort, in a coastline, a marriage, a chess opening and the queue at a supermarket. Once a reader has the word, everything begins to look one-sided. That is the failure mode, and the framework names it openly: the charge against this chapter is not that the mathematics is wrong, but that the pattern is so general as to be uninformative.

The defence is a distinction between using a pattern and claiming it. Using the Möbius strip as a way of thinking about a horizon is legitimate and costs nothing; it is the same move a physicist makes when calling a wave a wave. Claiming that the horizon is a seam, in the sense that the framework’s physics requires it to be one, is a substantive claim that sits on the framework’s own grade. The two are separated on the page, and the separation is the difference between a suggestive essay and a system that has quietly stopped paying rent on its claims.

There is one more piece of honesty to add, and it is the one that keeps this chapter from floating away. The seam, as a mathematical object, is completely understood: the Möbius strip, Stokes’ theorem, the holographic principle, the handedness of a twist. The seam as a claim about cells and moments of experience is not mathematics at all. It is a proposal that the same shape recurs in biology and in consciousness, and proposals of that shape are cheap. What is not cheap is this: the half-twist is the only object in the framework that can be made. You can hold it. You can cut it and count the results. Very little in this territory lets you do that, and it is a good reason for the framework to keep the half-twist at the centre of its imagery — not because the picture is beautiful, but because, unlike most of the rest, it can be tested with scissors.
