How Far Did It Get?

A scorecard for the dark sector — the framework’s attempt to turn two unknowns into one parameter. Two clean wins are claimed and one failure is conceded in the same breath, which is the point of the essay.

In this essay

  3. Win two: the flatness of the spin
  4. The failure: the hundred-and-twenty zeros

Two substances, or one?

Here is the embarrassment at the centre of modern cosmology, stated without jargon. When astronomers weigh the universe — count what it does, see how it moves, add up what must be there — the arithmetic does not close. The stuff we can see and touch and measure: stars, gas, dust, planets, us. That is about five per cent. Then something else, about a quarter of it, which pulls on galaxies as though it were matter but does not shine, and does not collide with itself, and has never been caught in anything. Then the remaining seventy per cent, which does not pull at all but pushes: the universe’s expansion is speeding up, and something is responsible. ◆

Physicists have names for the two mysteries — dark matter and dark energy — and, being honest people, they will tell you the names are labels for ignorance rather than discoveries. What they will also tell you is that the labels are convenient, and that convenience has hardened into a picture: two substances, sitting side by side in the equations, each with its own properties, each unexplained. The fact that their densities are comparable today — that we happen to live at the moment when one is not vanishingly small against the other — is called the coincidence problem, and it is genuinely strange.

The χ-field — the chi-field, the framework’s name for its own answer — offers a different picture, and it is a picture rather than a substance. Instead of two stuffs, one field, with a value that changes as conditions change. The value, written χ, is not an amount of something. It is more like a temperature: a way of describing the state of a region rather than a thing sitting in it. Under this reading, what we call dark matter is the field in one phase and what we call dark energy is the same field in another — the way ice and steam are not two substances but two states of the same one. The framework assigns no new particles and no new forces. It asks whether one continuous parameter can replace two unexplained nouns.

That is the claim of this essay’s chapter, and this essay is the chapter’s scorecard. A framework that makes a claim like that should expect to be marked, and marked in public. So: two wins, one failure, one open question. In that order.

Win one: the Bullet Cluster

The best evidence that dark matter is real — and the hardest thing for any alternative to explain — comes from a collision between two galaxy clusters, about 3.4 billion light years away, called the Bullet Cluster by everyone who has ever had to say its catalogue number out loud. ◆

What happened there is worth following carefully, because it is a genuine test rather than a description. Two clusters of galaxies ran into one another. Galaxy clusters are mostly three things: a handful of galaxies, a great deal of hot gas between them, and dark matter. The gas is the part that lights up and the part that interacts — it slams together, slows down, heats, and becomes a glowing bulge sitting right where the collision happened. The galaxies are mostly empty space and pass through each other like two crowds in a corridor. And the dark matter, whatever it is, appears not to collide with itself at all.

So run the tape forward. The gas piles up in the middle, glowing. The galaxies and the dark matter both sail through. Now look across the wreckage and ask: where is the matter that bends light? The answer, measured by gravitational lensing — the way the cluster’s gravity warps the background sky — is in two lumps on either side of the glowing gas, tracking the galaxies. The visible mass and the bending mass have come apart. Something invisible went through the collision without slowing down, and took most of the gravity with it.

Now the point for this essay. A theory in which dark matter is a substance has a story here: the substance is collisionless, so it separates. A theory in which the dark sector is geometry, or a modification of gravity, or an extra force that switches on at large distances, usually struggles badly, because those things are properties of space and space did not separate from itself. The χ-field gets this right, and it gets it right for a reason rather than by tuning: a field that is a state of matter and not matter itself can be differently affected by different things moving through it. The gas and the field do not ride together, because the gas and the field are not the same kind of object. ◆

This is the framework’s cleanest result, and it is worth being precise about what “clean” means. It does not mean the framework predicted the Bullet Cluster. It means that a class of explanations the framework belongs to — state, not substance — survives a test that kills a sibling class. That is a real elimination, and philosophy of science says eliminations are what happen when theories are young. It is not a confirmation. It is a win on points.

Win two: the flatness of the spin

The second win is subtler and, in the framework’s own accounting, more interesting: rotation curves.

Take a spiral galaxy. Count the visible matter — the stars in the disc, the gas, the dust. Work out how fast a galaxy with that much visible matter should be turning at a given distance from its centre, and you have a curve: fast near the middle, tapering off towards the edge, because beyond the visible material there should be little left to pull on. Now measure it. The curves do not taper. In galaxy after galaxy, the outer stars move just as fast as the inner ones — flat curves, out to where the measurements run out. Something is keeping the outer parts of galaxies moving, and it is not the stuff we can see. ◆

Explaining that flat curve is a standard test, and most dark-matter models pass it by putting more mass out there, in a halo. The framework passes it a different way. In the χ-field, the field varies — and if the field varies in a particular way as you move out through a galaxy, the effect on motion comes out flat. Which particular way? The chapter’s answer is: inversely with distance — χ going as one over r. Work that through, and the flat curve falls out of the geometry rather than needing to be arranged.

Here the honesty has to be sharpest. Two things are true at once. The fit is good, and the one-over-r choice is not arbitrary in the sense of being picked to make a graph look nice — it is a natural scale-free behaviour, and scale-free behaviour is what a flat curve demands. But it did not have to be one-over-r. The framework chose it, and the choice was made knowing what the answer had to look like. That is a fit. It is not a derivation, and the chapter says so in the sentence in which it takes the credit. ◆ A derived result would come with a reason that the field must behave that way, from something more basic. That reason has not been found.

The failure: the hundred-and-twenty zeros

Now the failure, which the framework does not hide and which is the most important paragraph in this essay.

There is a number in physics that everybody hates, and it is the measured density of dark energy. In natural units — the units you get when you set the constants of nature to one, which is what theorists do so that they are comparing like with like — the observed dark energy density is about 10−122. That is a one, a hundred and twenty-two places to the right of a decimal point, and then a value. Meanwhile, the naive quantum-field-theory estimate of how much energy empty space should hold comes out about 100 — order one — because quantum fields at every wavelength contribute. The two numbers differ by a hundred and twenty-two orders of magnitude. This is not a small discrepancy or a matter of the last decimal place. It is often called the worst prediction in the history of physics, and the description is fair. ◆

Does the χ-field fix it? No. Not remotely. The framework’s honest position is that the field can describe the dark sector’s behaviour — how it acts, how it splits in collisions, how it varies across a galaxy — without touching the question that produced the number. The hundred and twenty-two zeros are a problem about the vacuum, about where the value of the field comes from in the first place, and this framework has nothing to say about that. It does not derive the number. It does not explain why it is small. It does not reduce the discrepancy at all.

This is the concession that keeps the scorecard honest, and there is a way of reading it that makes the rest of the project more credible rather than less. Every alternative to the standard picture faces the same wall — the standard picture itself is destroyed by this number. Being one more theory that does not solve it is not a distinct embarrassment; pretending otherwise would be. The framework does not claim to have reduced the coincidence problem of dark matter and dark energy to one elegant parameter and solved the vacuum. It claims four things: dark matter and dark energy are one field, in two phases; collisions separate the field from gas; the field falls as one over r and gives flat curves; and the dark-energy density problem is untouched. Three wins, one refutation-pending. Any scorecard claiming otherwise would be selling something.

The open question the framework cannot yet answer

The fourth item on the scorecard is not a win or a loss. It is an open question, and it is the one a careful reader will have thought of already.

If the dark sector is one field with a value, what sets the value? In the model, the field’s density in a region follows from the surroundings — what is collapsing, what is spreading, what has been collapsing for long enough to have accumulated. The framework’s account of accumulation is the splice: deposits below the threshold of one add up into something measurable, the way temperature is nothing any single molecule has. That is the mechanism by which small things become big things. It is a good mechanism, and it is exact where it can be — the arithmetic of the splice is provable, and it is proved.

But between “the field accumulates where things have been collapsing” and “the field’s present value is 10−122” there is a chasm. Filling it would require something like a partition function for gravity: a way of counting how many ways a given arrangement can be built, the way thermodynamics counts how many ways molecules can be arranged, so that a temperature falls out. Statistical mechanics can do that for gas in a box, and it works beautifully. Nobody can do it for a universe. The framework offers the shape of the answer — accumulation, phases, thresholds — and does not have the machinery.

Two honest notes on that. First: no one else has the machinery either, and the appearance of progress in that direction is mostly notation. Second: this is exactly the kind of gap that the framework’s own stress tests were written to expose, and in those tests the concession is written in the framework’s own voice rather than extracted by an objector. That is the correct posture for a young programme. It is also, and this is the uncomfortable part, exactly what everyone says when they are stuck.

The scorecard, read plainly

So where does it stand? Four rows, no rounding up.

Read the picture left to right and the shape of a young theory is clear. It does well on behaviour — how the dark sector acts in collisions, how it falls off across a galaxy. It does badly on magnitude — how much there is, and why that much. Behaviour is easier: behaviours are constrained by observations, and the framework was built to look at observations. Magnitude is where the deep problems live, and the deep problems are untouched.

The most useful thing about this chapter is that it is a chapter at all. Frameworks usually do not score themselves in public, because the scorecard costs them more than it earns. This one does, because the alternative — quietly emphasising the two wins and hoping nobody asks about the third row — is a strategy with a short half-life. The dark-sector chapter is not the framework’s strongest ground. Its strongest ground is arithmetic, and that is where the next essay goes.

One more thing belongs on the scorecard, and it is a note about method rather than a result. The χ-field has one parameter, and one parameter is easy to fit to one curve. The test of a one-parameter model is not whether it fits something — almost anything fits something — but whether the same parameter, set once, survives several unrelated measurements. On the collision and on the rotation curves, the framework uses its parameter the same way, and the two uses agree. That is a real if modest achievement, and it is the sense in which the model is a model rather than a description. What it is not is a prediction. A prediction would say: go and look at this specific arrangement, and you will find a specific number that nobody has measured yet. The framework has a small number of those, they are listed in the stress tests, and none of them is in the dark sector. The dark sector is where the framework explains what is seen — and, whatever the third row of the scorecard says, it does that better than one would have any right to expect from a project that began with a dream about a wheel.

Read on: A Number Made of Itself takes the one part of this that is exact rather than fitted, and builds the framework’s safest claim out of it. If you would rather see the failure argued from the other side, the stress tests give the objectors their own paragraphs.
