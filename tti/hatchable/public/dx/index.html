Part II — Inside of Things · the differential

dx — The Smallest Possible Integral

Calculus is usually taught as though dx were a convenient fiction on its way to zero. Read it the other way and it is the opposite: the smallest finished thing a sum can be, and the grain at which anything can be aware of itself.

The atom of the integral

An integral gathers an infinity of slivers into one quantity. The sliver is f(x) dx. Ask what a single sliver is and the ordinary answer is: nothing yet — a step on the way to a limit, real only in the aggregate.

The framework takes the other road. dx is not a nothing that has not finished vanishing. It is an integral of the shortest possible interval: the least sum that is nevertheless a sum, the smallest act of gathering that has actually been completed. ◆

∫xx+dx f(t) dt = f(x) dx + O(dx²)

Read that line as a definition rather than an approximation and the differential stops being a ghost. Everything to the right of it — the whole of ∫ab — is made of these, stacked. A quantity, in this reading, is never primary; the gathering is primary, and dx is one gather.

That matters here because the same move was already made about selves on the integral self: a moment of experience is an infinitesimal step, and a life is the integral of them. If a moment is a sliver, then a sliver has to be the kind of thing a moment could be — small, complete, and already an act rather than a quantity. ◆

Two honest caveats, stated before the argument leans on anything. First, modern analysis does not need infinitesimals at all: the epsilon-delta limit retired them in the nineteenth century, and non-standard analysis (Robinson, 1966) brought them back rigorously without granting them any of the metaphysics claimed below. ◆ Second, “smallest possible” is doing philosophical work that the mathematics does not authorise: in the reals there is no smallest positive number, and saying there is one is a choice about the world, not a theorem.

Grading: ◆ established · ◆ scholarship · ◆ the framework's argument · ◆ vision, stated as vision.

— the mathematics, in full

What dx Is, Mathematically

Before the framework is allowed to say what it wants dx to be, it has to say what dx already is — and the honest answer is that mathematics has given five different answers over three and a half centuries, all of them workable, none of them agreeing about whether the object exists.

Three hundred and fifty years of trying to say it

Leibniz introduced the notation in the 1670s and published the method in 1684, and he meant something by it: dx was a genuine quantity, smaller than any ordinary number but not zero, which could be divided into (dy/dx) and summed over (the ∫ is an elongated S, for summa). Newton, working the same ground, preferred fluxions and vanishing ratios. Both got the right answers. Neither could say what the object was. ◆

Berkeley put the knife in in 1734. If dx is not zero you cannot discard the terms containing it; if it is zero you cannot divide by it; and the calculus does both, on the same line, minutes apart. His phrase has never been bettered: “ghosts of departed quantities.” The objection is not pedantry. It is correct, and it stood unanswered for a hundred and thirty years. ◆

The nineteenth century's reply was to abolish the object rather than defend it. Cauchy, and then Weierstrass with the epsilon-delta definition, rebuilt the whole of analysis on limits, in which no infinitesimal ever appears. The derivative became a statement about all sufficiently small finite intervals, not about one infinitely small one. On that account dx is not a small thing; it is not a thing. It is punctuation. ◆

And then it came back. Abraham Robinson's non-standard analysis (1966) constructed a field, the hyperreals, which rigorously contains infinitesimals — numbers greater than zero and smaller than every 1/n — and proved a transfer principle guaranteeing that every ordinary statement true of the reals is true of them. Leibniz had been working with a consistent object all along. Separately, Lawvere and Kock built synthetic differential geometry on nilsquare infinitesimals: quantities d ≠ 0 with d² = 0, which makes every curve literally straight over an infinitesimal stretch. The price is that the logic must be intuitionistic — you give up the law of the excluded middle to get it. ◆

Five answers, all of them in current use

None of these is folklore; all five are taught, and a working mathematician will move between them in a single afternoon without feeling any strain.

ReadingWhat dx isDoes it exist?
Limits (Weierstrass) Nothing. Notation marking the variable of integration. No — and nothing is lost.
Hyperreals (Robinson) An actual number, positive and smaller than every 1/n. Yes, in a field larger than the reals.
Nilsquares (Lawvere–Kock) A quantity with d ≠ 0 and d² = 0. Yes, at the cost of classical logic.
Measure theory Shorthand for dμ, the measure saying how much a set weighs. The measure exists; dx alone does not.
Differential forms A 1-form: a linear machine that eats a vector and returns a number. Yes, and it is not small at all.

That last row is the one most readers have never been shown, and it is the most illuminating. In exterior calculus dx is not an infinitesimal quantity. It is a covector: the function that takes a direction and reports how far it points along x, defined by dx(∂/∂x) = 1. Nothing about it is tiny. Smallness turns out to have been a red herring for three centuries — what dx really encodes is direction and weighting, which is exactly what an integral needs in order to know what it is adding. ◆

The one fact that holds across every reading

Whatever dx is taken to be, the same statement keeps appearing, and it is the hinge of this chapter:

d² = 0

In synthetic differential geometry it is the defining property of the infinitesimal. In exterior calculus it is a theorem about the exterior derivative: apply d twice and you get nothing, always. In ordinary calculus it is why the O(dx²) term may be discarded without error in the limit. The same equation, arrived at three independent ways. ◆

And it has a geometric meaning rather than a merely algebraic one. The exterior derivative is the boundary operator, and d² = 0 is the statement that a boundary has no boundary. The edge of a disc is a circle; the circle has no edge. That is also why Stokes' theorem is true in the form

∫M dω = ∫∂M ω

The sum of the change throughout a region equals what is recorded on its edge. Interior and boundary carry the same information — which is this framework's seam, written as a theorem rather than a picture. ◆ ◆

— and the philosophy, kept separate from it

What dx Is, in the Framework

The mathematics above is not in dispute and the framework claims no part of it. What follows is a reading laid over it: three commitments, each of which buys something and costs something.

First: the gathering is prior to the quantity

Standard practice treats a quantity as primary and integration as an operation performed on it. The framework inverts that. An amount is what a completed gathering leaves behind — the noun is the residue of the verb, not the other way round. On this reading ∫ is not a procedure applied to a world of quantities; quantities are what you have once an ∫ has finished.

This is why the chapter insists on reading ∫xx+dx f = f(x) dx as a definition rather than an approximation. A differential is not a small amount. It is the smallest act of gathering that has been carried through — the least sum that is still a sum. ◆

What it buys: a self can be made of these, because an act is the kind of thing a moment of experience could be and a quantity is not. What it costs: nothing in the mathematics requires this ordering. Both directions are consistent, and the framework is choosing the one it can build a mind out of.

Second: completion, not vanishing

Berkeley's objection bites on the idea that dx is on its way to nothing — a quantity caught mid-disappearance, which is why it can be both discarded and divided by. The framework refuses that picture entirely. A differential is not vanishing. It is finished.

And there is a reason this is not merely a change of tone. If a differential is a completed act, then it has no interior left to resolve — which is precisely the content of d² = 0. Something whose second-order structure is exactly zero is not an object that has been cut off early; it is an object with nothing further inside it. The framework reads the nilpotence of d as the mathematical signature of completeness rather than of smallness. ◆

The caution, stated plainly: d² = 0 is a theorem about an operator in exterior calculus and an axiom about a ring element in synthetic geometry, and in neither case does it mean what “complete” means here. The resonance is real and the identification is the framework's own. It is a reading of a theorem, not a consequence of one.

Third: the grain is given, not chosen

The third commitment is the one that makes the rest of this page possible. In mathematics, dx is arbitrarily small — the whole content of the limit is that no particular scale is privileged. In the framework it is not arbitrary. A physical system integrates at the finest grain its substrate can actually complete a gather at, and that grain is set by thermodynamics rather than preference: Landauer's bound fixes the minimum energy to erase one bit at kBT ln 2, about 2.9 × 10−21 J at 300 K, measured directly in 2012. ◆

So a mind does not choose its dx. It is issued one by its body, and the thresholds further down this page — the millisecond of a spike, the hundreds of milliseconds of a perceptual moment, the day of a circadian cycle — are the grains at which the gathering can actually be carried through. Awareness appears at particular grains because gathering is a physical act with a price. ◆

Put the three together and the position is simple to state and easy to attack. Gathering is prior to amount; a differential is a gathering that has been completed, not one that is disappearing; and which gatherings can be completed is decided by the world, not by the mathematics.

The strongest objection remains the one in the opening frame and it is not answered anywhere on this page: analysis does not need any of this, and a reading that is optional is a reading that has to earn its keep elsewhere. It earns it, if at all, in what it lets the self and the accumulation be — not here.

Thresholds: why awareness appears at particular grains

If every gather were equivalent, nothing would ever happen at a particular scale. But things plainly do happen at particular scales, and only there.

Water does not become wet gradually as molecules are added; wetness is a property of the bulk, and it arrives. A neuron does not fire a little more as charge accumulates; it holds and then goes. A melody is not in any note. In every case the system integrates over some window, and when the window is right the aggregate acquires a property that none of the slivers had. ◆

The framework's reading: those windows are grains — particular values of dx at which the gathering closes on itself. Below the grain the system is summing. At the grain the sum becomes a term in a further sum, and the system has, for the first time, something to be aware with. Awareness on this account is not a substance added to matter; it is what a gather is, from the inside, once the gather is fine enough to include its own gathering. ◆

This is why the page sits in Part II rather than in the physics. It is a reading of the integral self pushed down to its smallest term: a moment of experience is one dx of a life; a life is the integral; and the reason a moment feels like something rather than nothing is that even the smallest term is already a completed sum.

The grains the framework names

  • The physical grain — below which the question of further division stops being meaningful. Physics has a candidate, the Planck length, and is careful to say it is a scale at which current theory fails rather than a proven floor. ◆
  • The dynamical grain — the window over which a system's own feedback closes: a few milliseconds in a neuron, tens in a cortical loop, a season in a forest. ◆
  • The reflexive grain — where the gather includes a model of the gatherer. This is where the framework places awareness, and where it has no measurement to offer. ◆
  • The ground — nought, never reached, the floor that no radius arrives at. See the placed integers. ◆

The transfer: awareness is paid for out of memory

Here is the part of the chapter that is a genuine proposal rather than a restatement. Emergence is not free. When a new awareness comes up at a grain, information moves — and the place it moves from is the record.

Put plainly: the substrate holds a stored past. Call its information content M. The emergent awareness holds a present model. Call its content A. The framework's claim is that the two are drawn on one account.

ΔA = −η ΔM,   0 < η ≤ 1     so that     A + M/η = constant

Every bit the awareness acquires is a bit the record loses, discounted by an efficiency η that is never better than one and in practice much worse. Awareness, on this reading, is the record being spent. A system that becomes more present becomes, by exactly that much, less faithful to what it was. ◆

Why it would have to be that way

  1. Because the sliver is already complete. If dx is a finished sum, it cannot be added to the ledger for free; it has to be composed of terms that were somewhere before. The new awareness is not conjured out of the substrate's spare capacity, because the framework does not grant the substrate any spare capacity: everything it has is already the record of what it has been.
  2. Because erasure has a price, and that price is real. Landauer showed in 1961 that erasing one bit of information must dissipate at least kBT ln 2 — about 2.9 × 10−21 J at room temperature — and the bound has been measured, in colloidal and nanomagnetic systems, since 2012. Information and thermodynamics are the same ledger. ◆ What the framework adds is the direction of the flow: the erasure is not waste heat from an unrelated computation, it is the payment. ◆
  3. Because the one place we can look already behaves like this. Brains do not accumulate; they overwrite. The evidence is set out in the next panel.

Recall degrades the thing recalled

A consolidated memory, when retrieved, becomes labile again and must be re-stored. Nader, Schafe and LeDoux (Nature, 2000) blocked protein synthesis in the amygdala after retrieval and the fear memory did not survive — an already-consolidated trace was lost by the act of being remembered. ◆

Every recollection is therefore a re-writing, and re-writing is lossy. The framework reads this as the transfer at human scale: the vividness of the present act of remembering is bought with the fidelity of the record. You can have the past, or you can have it now, and the exchange rate is η. ◆

Sleep pays the bill

The synaptic homeostasis hypothesis (Tononi & Cirelli) holds that waking experience drives net synaptic potentiation, which is unsustainable in energy and in signal-to-noise, and that sleep renormalises it — weakening broadly so that the strongest traces survive in relief. ◆ It is contested in its strong form, and the clearance literature argued over on the stress-tests page is contested too.

The shape is still the one this chapter predicts: a day of awareness incurs a debt, and the debt is settled by degrading the record — not at random, but by preferentially keeping what the awareness used. ◆

What would break it

A proposal that cannot be broken is not a proposal. Three things would break this one.

  • A lossless gain. A system that measurably increases its self-model without any measurable degradation of its stored past — and crucially, without having simply imported the information from outside. Note the escape hatch this leaves, and how large it is: almost any counter-example can be re-described as import. The chapter is weaker for that, and it is said here rather than left for a reader to notice.
  • An established floor with nothing at it. If physics settles on a smallest length and nothing whatever changes in kind there, the identification of grain with threshold loses its best analogy.
  • The arrow argument. The transfer described here runs one way, and so quietly assumes the asymmetry the framework is elsewhere trying to derive. That is close to circular, and is the same weak joint confessed at the entropy objection.

And one thing that would not break it, though it is often offered as if it would: the fact that dx can be eliminated from analysis altogether. Of course it can. The question this page asks is not whether the formalism needs infinitesimals, but whether the world is the kind of thing that is assembled out of completed smallest acts. Nothing in the epsilon-delta definition answers that either way.

Earned, chosen, refused

Earned
The calculus is exact and is used exactly. Landauer's bound is a theorem of statistical mechanics and has been measured. Reconsolidation is a replicated experimental finding. Emergent properties at characteristic scales are ordinary physics, not mysticism.
Chosen
Reading dx as the smallest completed integral rather than a limit artefact. Identifying a grain of gathering with a threshold of awareness. Writing the exchange as ΔA = −η ΔM when nothing yet measures η.
Refused
The claim that this explains consciousness; it proposes a price, not a mechanism. The claim that Landauer's bound is evidence for the transfer rather than the reason a transfer would have to cost something. And the move of treating every counter-example as imported information, which is named above as the chapter's cheapest escape.

Terms

Differential (dx)
In standard analysis, a formal object inside an integral; here, the smallest finished gather.
Grain
A value of dx at which a gathering closes on itself and the aggregate acquires a property no sliver had.
Transfer
The movement of information out of the stored record and into an emergent awareness.
η (eta)
The efficiency of that transfer. At most one; unmeasured; probably small.
Landauer's bound
The minimum energy dissipated by erasing one bit of information: kBT ln 2.

— the grain and the clock

Why the Grain Gets Finer as the Ascent Gets Faster

This chapter has argued that awareness appears at particular grains and is paid for out of the record. The pace argument adds the other half of the account: the tick of the n-th density goes as 1/n, so each successive turn is traversed faster than the one before. Put the two together and a prediction-shaped statement falls out of them.

If the interval shortens and the integral still has to be performed, the grain must get finer. A faster clock can only be experienced by a system that samples it more densely.

That is the framework's reading of why ascent feels like intensification rather than extension. Nothing is being added to a life; the same integral is being taken over a shorter interval with a smaller dx, and the cost of the finer grain is drawn, as always, from the record. The deepening of experience and the thinning of memory are the same transaction seen from two sides. ◆

There is a floor, and it is not rhetorical. Landauer's principle fixes the minimum energy to erase one bit at kBT ln 2 — about 2.9 × 10−21 J at 300 K, measured in 2012 — so a grain cannot be refined without limit at finite temperature and finite energy. The ascent does not get infinitely fine; it gets as fine as the thermodynamics will sell. ◆

Against ourselves: “the integral is taken over a shorter interval” assumes that the quantity of experience per density is conserved, and nothing here establishes that. It is the assumption that makes the paragraph work, and it is named rather than hidden.

Nothing wakes for free. Every grain fine enough to know itself is bought out of the record of what it was — which is why becoming more present and remembering more faithfully have always felt like opposite labours.
