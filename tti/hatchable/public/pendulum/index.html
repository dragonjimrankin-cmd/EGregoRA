The Paper · after Foundations · the pendulum, doubled

The Pendulum

The universe is an eternal integration of opposing infinities which together swing like a double pendulum. That is the sentence this paper was written to test.

Graded throughout as the rest of the site is graded: ◆ established · ◆ scholarship · ◆ the framework's argument · ◆ vision, stated as vision.

The Sentence

The universe is an eternal integration of opposing infinities which together swing like a double pendulum.

Four load-bearing words and one comparison. If the comparison is doing any work at all, each of them must be answerable to something you can hold in one hand — a weight on a string.

✦

I — the sentence, and what it owes

A Claim of This Shape Is Cheap

Any sentence containing eternal and infinity can be made to sound true. The ear supplies the proof. So the only useful thing to do with a claim of this shape is to take it apart and ask of each piece what it could be wrong about: what observation, what arithmetic, what experiment would have to come out otherwise.

The pieces are these. Eternal — the motion never stops. Integration — something is being summed, continuously, and the sum is not a term in it. Opposing infinities — the motion is bounded by two unreachable limits, not by walls. Double — one pendulum will not do; the sentence needs two arms or it is describing a clock. And the swing, which is what all five of those turn into when you stop talking and release the weight.

One of those pieces turns out to be false as usually read, and the correction is the most interesting thing in the paper. We will come to it at IV. First, the two that are simply true.

✦

II — the swing is a turn, seen edge-on

The Shadow on the Real Axis

Hang a weight from a string, pull it aside a little and let go. For small displacements the motion is

θ(t) = θ₀ cos(ωt),   ω = √(g/L)

and cos(ωt) is the real part of eiωt. That is not a flourish; it is the same equation written twice. The pendulum’s swing is what a rotation looks like when you can only see the real axis: a body going round a circle of which only the horizontal diameter has been cut out and shown to you. The bob is not going round anything, of course. But the quantity that describes it is, and the description is exact. ◆

This is the Foundations chain arriving in the hand. Halving π gives i, and eiπ/2 = i is a quarter-turn: the axis along which the rotation happens is not decorative but load-bearing, because a pendulum’s equation is only solvable in closed form at all when you allow yourself that axis. Strip the imaginary part out and you are left with a differential equation that still has an answer, but no longer has a turn in it, and so no longer has a period in it either — only a decay, or a runaway. The period is the imaginary part, brought back.

Everything in this section is arithmetic. The sentence it supports — that the real world is the shadow of a complex one — is the framework’s reading, and it is marked as a reading wherever it appears.

✦

III — the return is priced in π

A Number You Can Time With Your Thumb

Cut a metre of string, tie a weight to it, and time ten swings. What you will measure, if the swing is small, agrees with

T = 2π√(L/g) ≈ 2.0 s

which is to say that π arrives not as a symbol but as a number you can reach with a stopwatch and a thumb. A period is a return — that is all it is — and the constant of every return is π, so the constant is in the period. On the site’s reading, this is Plate I made physical: π as the condition of there being a closed path at all. ◆

Now hold the bob up near the horizontal and let go, and the agreement fails — the period lengthens, and the further you go the worse the small-angle formula does. Push the release angle towards the top and the period does not approach some larger finite number. It diverges. There is no period at all: the bob takes forever to climb the last part of the arc, and forever is not a long time, it is no time.

That is the first appearance of the shape the next section needs. A pendulum has two places it can be aimed at, and only one of them can ever be reached.

✦

IV — where the two infinities actually are

Not the Turning Points. The Two Places It Can Rest.

The obvious reading of the sentence is that the two infinities are the two ends of the swing — the extremities, left and right, that the bob never passes. The obvious reading is wrong, and it is worth saying so plainly rather than building on it. The turning points are not infinities. The bob visits them, twice a period, exactly, with no trouble at all. What is infinite about them is nothing; what is zero about them is the velocity, and zero is the other infinity, not this one.

Look instead for the places the pendulum cannot reach, and there are exactly two, because a pendulum has exactly two rest points.

The bottom. Directly below the pivot the pendulum hangs still. It is a stable rest: displace it and it comes back. Everything falls towards it. Add the smallest damping — and every real pendulum has damping, the same second law that gives this site its arrow of time — and every swing ends there. But it does not arrive there. The amplitude decays geometrically, halving and halving, and each half takes longer to become visible than the last: the rest position is approached forever and never occupied. This is the framework’s zero exactly — the floor no shrinking quantity reaches, the infinity that opens downward into the small. ◆

The top. Directly above the pivot, balanced. It is an unstable rest: displace it and it falls away, one way or the other, and which way is decided by the last decimal place. It is also a threshold, and this is the part that matters. Below it, the bob swings and turns back; above it, the bob goes over the top and circles for ever in one direction. The single trajectory that separates the two regimes is the separatrix, and a pendulum released exactly onto it climbs towards the vertical and never arrives. Not slowly — never: the period is infinite. This is the framework’s one, and the agreement is closer than a likeness. One is the edge of the measurable, the threshold of this rather than that. The separatrix is nothing else: it is the line between this kind of motion and that kind, which is a threshold, and it is unreachable, which is what makes it an infinity. ◆

So the sentence’s two infinities are the pendulum’s two equilibria — the ground it can never settle onto, and the threshold it can never cross — and the integration is the arc between them, run forwards and back, forever, which is what an eternal swing is.

None of this geometry is new, and none of it is the framework’s. It is drawn, already, as Plate II of Foundations: the phase portrait, the two ringed points at ±π that are the inverted position, and the gold curve whose note reads a pendulum released exactly onto it approaches the vertical and never reaches it. The period is infinite. The framework’s contribution is not the geometry. It is the claim that those two points are what the site has been calling zero and one since the first plate, and that reading is chosen, not earned — an interpretation laid over an established portrait, and offered as one.

✦

V — why the sentence has to say double

The Most Law-Governed Thing in the Room

A single pendulum is the most predictable object in classical physics. It is how we kept time for three centuries. Nobody has ever described a clock as out of control, and nobody should: if the sentence meant chaos, one arm would not carry it.

Hang a second pendulum from the bob of the first and everything changes, though no law does. Two arms, two angles, two equations — and their configuration space is, quite literally, the torus this site draws elsewhere: one angle round the first circle, one round the second, and the pair of them a point on the surface of a doughnut. ◆ The equations remain exact. The future remains exact. What is lost is not determinism but tractability: two double pendulums released a thousandth of a radian apart lie on the same curve for twenty seconds, are visibly apart at thirty, and share nothing but the law by the end of the first minute — which is the figure running in your browser at the foot of this section. Littlewood, in his Miscellany, did the same arithmetic on the back of an envelope for a single pendulum, where the divergence takes minutes rather than seconds; the second arm buys the metaphor its unpredictability and costs exactly that difference. ◆

Say clearly what chaos is, because the sentence needs it and the word is abused. The double pendulum is not lawless; it is the opposite of lawless. It is so tightly governed that an error of one part in a thousand becomes an error of everything, and the reason it defeats prediction is that we cannot know its starting position well enough, not that there is nothing to know. An out-of-control pendulum is the most law-governed thing in the room. That is a strange comfort and it is the true one — but it is also the point at which out of control stops being physics and becomes a mood, and the sentence should be graded accordingly: the unpredictability is ◆, the adjective is ◆.

There is one more gift in the double pendulum, and it is the reason the site’s torus and the site’s chaos are not rivals. Swing it gently and it is not chaotic at all: at small amplitudes the two arms are two coupled oscillators, the motion is integrable, and the system winds endlessly around invariant tori without ever repeating and without ever diverging — quasiperiodic, ordered, endless. Turn the energy up and most of those tori survive, deformed; chaos appears in the gaps between them and widens as the energy rises. That is the content of the Kolmogorov–Arnold–Moser theorem, and it is a theorem. ◆ Order on the tori; unpredictability between them; the same object, all the way up. ◆

VI — the free gift: Huygens, February 1665

Sympathy: The First Time Anyone Watched Two Things Become One Time

Christiaan Huygens was ill in bed in the winter of 1665, and had hung two of his own pendulum clocks on the same wooden beam near him so he could watch them. He noticed that they had fallen into step: swinging always in opposition, and keeping exact time with one another. He disturbed them. Within half an hour they were locked again. He wrote to his father about it, and to correspondents, and gave the phenomenon a name he must have enjoyed — sympathia, the sympathy of clocks. ◆

It is the earliest recorded observation of mutual synchronisation between coupled oscillators, and it is this paper’s free gift, because it is the piece the pendulum argument was missing. Huygens had, nine years earlier, built the first reliable pendulum clock — the instrument that made time countable and therefore measurable, and so the ancestor of every ledger on this site. In 1665 he accidentally showed what happens when two such instruments are joined by a common support: the beam carries a whisper of each swing into the other, and the two oscillators become, in a limited but real sense, one oscillator. Neither of them changes its law. What changes is that the pair now has a joint behaviour that neither had alone.

This is a mature physics now, and it is worth being precise about how mature. Coupled oscillators lock; that is measured in firefly flashes, in lasers, in pacemaker cells, in bridge crowds, in the applause of an audience, and in the mathematics of Kuramoto’s model from 1975. Huygens’s own setup was not fully accounted for until it was modelled properly in 2002. None of that is in question. ◆ ◆

And here is the bridge, which the framework does not get for free. The route from this paper to The Megatubule is that a lattice of coupled oscillators is exactly what synchronised time-keeping is — the mechanism by which many things with their own periods become one thing with a shared one. If a self is an integral, then synchronisation is the most ordinary physical means by which an integral is taken: not a metaphor for gathering many moments into one but the engineering of it. The physics of synchronisation is established. The identification of the microtubule lattice’s coherence with experience remains the framework’s argument, it carries its objections on its own page, and the decoherence objection is not weakened by anything in this paper. ◆

VII — what keeps it swinging

An Eternal Swing Needs Something Supplying It

A swing does not last. Damping is not a detail bolted onto the pendulum problem; it is the physics of every real one, and its arrow is the site’s arrow. An eternal swing therefore requires a driver, and a driver requires a gradient to fall down.

The framework’s answer is the one already given on the Gravity page: the gradient is the fall towards the denser infinity at zero, and what the swing looks like when the fall overshoots. An oscillator that overshoots and returns is exactly what a driven pendulum is; the universe does not settle because nothing has stopped supplying the drop. That is the framework’s claim about the universe, not a result about pendulums, and this is the honest place to say where the paper stops being able to check itself. A pendulum can demonstrate a driven swing. It cannot demonstrate that the driver is curvature. ◆

✦

VIII — the standing rule

What Is Earned and What Is Chosen

Earned

  • θ(t) = θ₀cos(ωt) = Re eiωt: the swing is the real shadow of a rotation. ◆
  • T = 2π√(L/g) at small amplitude, and the period diverges as the release angle approaches the top. ◆
  • A pendulum has exactly two equilibria; the separatrix between swinging and turning over has infinite period. ◆
  • The double pendulum’s configuration space is a torus; its motion is deterministic and, away from the integrable limit, sensitively dependent on initial conditions. ◆
  • KAM: near the integrable limit most invariant tori survive deformation, and chaos lives in the gaps between them. ◆
  • Huygens, 1665: two clocks on one beam fall into step. The first recorded observation of mutual entrainment; modelled properly in 2002. ◆

Chosen, and refused

  • Chosen: that the pendulum’s two rest points are the framework’s two infinities — the ground it never settles onto as zero, the threshold it never crosses as one. An interpretation laid over an established phase portrait. ◆
  • Chosen: that the cosmic swing is the integration taken between them, and that coupling is what turns many periods into one time. ◆
  • Chosen: “out of control” as an image of the double pendulum’s unpredictability. ◆
  • Refused: the reading that the two infinities are the turning points. They are visited twice per period. The image is prettier and it is wrong, and it would have cost the sentence its meaning.
  • Refused: the suggestion that a pendulum proves anything about the universe. It illustrates, and it connects; it does not extend. What is testable on this site is on the Seams and Stress Tests pages.
  • Refused: the popular flourish that π is in the period because the universe favours circles. π is in the period because a period is a return, and every return is priced in π.
✦

The sentence, kept

The universe is an eternal integration of opposing infinities which together swing like a double pendulum.

It survives, with one word added and one word qualified. Double, because a single pendulum is a clock and the sentence is not describing a clock. And “out of control” only in the sense that a double pendulum is beyond our control of its prediction — never in the sense that it is beyond law.

What the metaphor now carries, having been taken apart and put back, is this: a swing is a rotation seen edge-on; the return is priced in π; the two infinities are the two places it can rest, one of which it can never settle onto and the other of which it can never cross; the integration is the arc between them; two arms are needed for the future to be unpredictable while remaining exact; and coupling is how separate swings become one time. Nothing there is decoration, and nothing there is new except the arrangement. ◆

A reader who wants the original sentence unaltered may have it. It is a fine sentence. It is just no longer doing what it looked like it was doing, and the difference between those two things is the whole of the method.
